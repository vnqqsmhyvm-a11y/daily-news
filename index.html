Tulsa and Oklahoma

The hotel tax is the city money question on the Nov. 3 Tulsa ballot, so here are the city’s own numbers. Proposition 1 would nearly double Tulsa’s lodging tax, from 5% to 9.9%, starting Jan. 1, 2027. The council says the current rate brought in $10.4 million in 2026 and the new rate would bring in about $20.6 million. The first use is $80 million in revenue bonds for the BOK Center and the Arvest Convention Center, adding up to $128 million in projects once $48 million in reserves, money from Oak View Group (the company that runs both arenas) and Improve Our Tulsa 3 is counted. More marketing money only comes in 2031. Who benefits is clear: the arena operator, the convention business and Visit Tulsa, who were the main presenters at the council’s hearings. Visitors pay most of it. Tulsans pay it too on any local hotel stay under 30 days, and short-term rentals with five or more rooms are covered. The “not on residents” pitch is mostly true, but the debt is real. If hotel bookings fall short, the bonds still have to be paid, and that risk is not in the city’s FAQ. This is the first increase in more than 40 years (Tulsa City Council).

Wednesday’s council agenda has $65.66 million in second-reading appropriations from bond money already approved: $26.32 million from the 2020 bond for streets, sidewalks and bridges, and $39.34 million from the 2024 bond for streets, alleys, sidewalks and the Performing Arts Center. That is spending voters already approved, not new debt. Also up for a final vote, with an emergency clause, is Councilor Hall-Harper’s ordinance setting rules for human remains and artifacts tied to racial violence, including a review panel that would weigh in on city projects and city-regulated development (filed agenda). Watch the review panel’s real power over private projects, since an emergency clause means there is no waiting period.

On license-plate cameras, the Tulsa World reports that Tulsa police and the sheriff now run different search rules [paywalled, headline only]. The police policy posted in October requires a documented investigative search and a teletype confirmation before a stop. The sheriff still allows 30-day history searches by plate or vehicle description with a case number, and he is defending that in court after a federal judge in the Northern District ruled the 30-day search unconstitutional without probable cause (TPD policy; News On 6). The city tightened its rules and the county didn’t, and that difference is the story.

The Justice Department told the State Election Board it will send monitors to Tulsa, Oklahoma and Muskogee counties on Nov. 3 to check compliance with the Voting Rights Act, the motor-voter law and the accessibility law. Locations and headcounts haven’t been released, and the monitors won’t interfere at the polls (Oklahoma Voice). The reporting cites no specific local complaint behind the choice of counties. Early voting runs Wednesday through Friday from 8 to 6 and Saturday from 8 to 2.

Iran, and what the calendar is telling you

Over the weekend Trump said it’s “getting to be decision time” between a deal and “finish it off,” and also said “there won’t be a strike today.” CNN, citing two U.S. officials, reports that forces in the region can launch major operations within 96 hours of an order, and that the plans go well beyond hitting launchers and radars again. The last confirmed U.S. strikes on Iran were Sept. 1 (CNN; CNBC). That is a third carrier group moving in, which is an action, set against no order and no strike, which is the absence of one. The incentive is plain: hold off until after the midterm, then decide in the window before the new Congress is seated. Ambassador Barrack’s warning of a weekend strike did not happen.

What actually happened on the diplomatic side: Saudi Arabia’s defense minister, Pakistan’s army chief and Turkey’s intelligence chief met Iran’s president in Tehran. These are the three Mecca Pact countries, which signed a mutual-defense pact in August. Iranian state media says they agreed to keep talking, and Iran says its nuclear program is not on the table. Qatari mediators were in Tehran on Sunday in coordination with Washington. Meanwhile the Houthis claim they hit pumping stations on the Saudi East-West pipeline and the Riyadh airport. Riyadh hasn’t confirmed that, and the Saudi-led coalition says it shot down three drones. Arab press reports of a “breakthrough this week” are single-source, so treat them as speculation.

Money, oil and the Fed

December Brent settled at $115.81, down 91 cents, and WTI at $101.76, down $1.51. Both are up about 12% this month, and Brent briefly topped $117 (WSJ; CNBC). The number to keep in mind is the Strategic Petroleum Reserve at 282.6 million barrels, its lowest since 1982 (Newsquawk; EIA’s weekly table not checked). The government’s emergency cushion is mostly spent going into a possible second round of fighting. A trader’s estimate of a roughly $20 war premium is opinion, not a measurement.

The 10-year Treasury held at 4.99% and the 2-year rose 3 basis points to 4.71%. The $69 billion 2-year auction came in soft, clearing a little above the market yield, which means buyers wanted a bit more to take the debt (WSJ). Futures show about a 90% chance the Fed holds at 3.75–4.00% on Wednesday, so the news will be what Chairman Warsh says about more hikes. PCE inflation comes Thursday. Stocks rose anyway, with the S&P up 0.7% to 7,676.77, led by Nvidia (AP). Gold is about $4,014 and bitcoin about $79,800, both flat on the day. Neither moved on policy, so there’s no bitcoin section tonight.
