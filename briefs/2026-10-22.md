**Tulsa and Oklahoma.**

**A Republican lawmaker told the Senate that Oklahoma's plate-reader cameras are being used unlawfully.** At an Oct. 21 interim study led by Sen. Mary Boren (D-Norman), Rep. Tom Gann (R-Inola) pointed to the only state statute on automated license-plate readers, which says the data can't be used "for purposes other than enforcement of the Compulsory Insurance Law or as otherwise permitted by law." Read at face value, Gann said, the way agencies use them now is unlawful. He said he wants a bill next session to ban government use of the cameras for persistent tracking, arguing that "a month long, multi-state, searchable database of a person's vehicle movement is not plain view." Police chiefs from The Village and Edmond defended the cameras. The Village chief said each search needs a case number and a reason, that his department doesn't share data outside Oklahoma, and that cutting Flock's seven-day default retention much further would hurt cases detectives pick up late. This follows the Oct. 1 federal ruling out of a Tulsa County stop that 30 days of plate tracking was a warrantless search. An interim study changes no law. The bill text, if one is filed for the February session, is the thing to watch, along with who votes against it. (Senate meeting notice, primary; KFOR; News 9. Fact / stated intent.)

**Tulsa kept its downtown juvenile curfew for another year.** The council voted unanimously Wednesday to extend the rule barring unaccompanied minors from downtown from 9 p.m. to 6 a.m. on Fridays and Saturdays. The city's own count shows 20 citations from Oct. 22, 2025, through Sept. 1, 2026. Fifteen went to Black youth, and eight came in one two-hour stretch on Feb. 7. Supporters cite a summer with no gun violence downtown, a claim attributed to the police department and not one we've seen backed by a published report. Twenty tickets in ten months means the ordinance works mostly as a tool police can use, not one they use often. A separate Oklahoma Natural Gas franchise ordinance is still pending. (Tulsa Flyer; council agenda, primary. Fact.)

**State prisons are hiring about as fast as they lose people.** At a House interim study Thursday, Corrections Director Justin Farris said the department has hired more than 3,300 people since 2023 and lost more than 3,100, a net gain of 194, with 641 jobs open. Housing units that once had two or three officers now usually have one. The department's HR director said mid-tier officers earn 18% to 21.7% less than peers in Arkansas, Colorado, Kansas, Missouri, New Mexico and Texas. This is the opening of a pay-raise ask for the February session. No dollar figure is on the table yet, so watch for one in the budget request. (Oklahoma House, primary account of the hearing. Fact / agency ask.)

**National and foreign.**

**The U.S. is pulling non-emergency staff out of Saudi Arabia, and Brent jumped 4%.** The State Department ordered non-emergency employees and families to leave, saying Houthi attacks on airports and infrastructure "have the potential to escalate rapidly." Saudi Arabia's energy ministry said debris from an intercepted drone aimed at the Shaybah oil field killed two contract workers, and that operations weren't affected. A strike on Dammam's airport killed at least one person and injured eight. Death tolls differ between BBC and Arab News, so treat them as preliminary. The Houthis also claimed a strike on Aramco's Yanbu site, which Saudi Arabia hasn't confirmed. Yanbu matters because it's the Red Sea outlet Saudi Arabia has used since Hormuz effectively closed. Iran's Revolutionary Guard navy claimed seven more vessels hit in the Gulf and warned that U.S. moves to escort shipping could bring strikes on U.S. bases. That is a claim, not a verified count. December Brent settled at $113.17, up $4.35 and its highest since May 4. WTI rose $2.32 to $99.47, and diesel futures rose about 17 cents to $4.93 a gallon. Pull diplomats' families out and you're telling the market you don't expect this to calm down soon. (State Department statement via BBC; Saudi energy ministry via Arab News; DTN; Dow Jones; Newsquawk. Fact / unverified claims labeled.)

**Economics and geopolitics.**

**France now pays more to borrow than Washington does.** French 10-year yields hit a 24-year high above 5%, nearly 1.8 points over Germany at the intraday peak. That's the widest gap since the 2011 euro crisis. The causes are a large deficit, a stalled government, and the worst street unrest in years. Belgium's borrowing costs hit their highest since 2012, and Italian yields rose 14 basis points this week. Protesters across Europe are naming fuel, cost of living and housing. That's what years of deficits plus an energy shock look like when the bond market stops waiting. (Reuters. Fact / analyst opinion.)

**U.S. Treasuries got the safe-haven money.** The 10-year fell about 17 basis points to roughly 4.96%, its biggest one-day drop since March 2023, after hitting 5.36% last week. The 2-year fell to about 4.65%. Tech led stocks lower after Meta's earnings: the Nasdaq fell 2.6% and the S&P 500 fell 1.2%, while the Dow finished flat. Meta's five-year credit-default swaps, the price of insuring its debt, hit a record, which tells you the AI spending boom is now being priced as credit risk, not just stock risk. Futures moved toward the Fed holding instead of raising rates once more this year, but another hike is still priced as more likely. Gold fell almost 3% to about $4,020–4,040 and silver fell about 5%, even as yields dropped. That looks like selling to raise cash, though the price action alone can't prove why. (Newsquawk; investingLive; Capp Notes; CoinDesk. Fact / interpretation labeled.)

**Bitcoin** fell about 3–4% to roughly $81,200, moving with tech stocks rather than against them. There was no policy or protocol news behind the move. (CoinDesk; investingLive.)
