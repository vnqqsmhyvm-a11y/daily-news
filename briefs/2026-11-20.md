**Tulsa and Oklahoma.**

**Nichols floats a 2027 vote on downtown money.** In his State of the City speech Thursday, Mayor Monroe Nichols pitched Project Nexus, a downtown redevelopment approaching $1 billion, along with a convention-center hotel, the old Evans-Fintube site and Kirkpatrick Heights. Afterward he said a bond package is one option and that his goal is to put something before voters in 2027. No funding plan, election date or ballot language exists yet. He also said he is not proposing to renew Improve Our Tulsa or Vision next year (FOX23 via KRMG). The figures he used are his own projections, not results: 5,500 jobs "announced" in 2026, about 150 conventions lost a year without a hotel, and $1.6 billion in economic activity from that hotel. He says there's "a pathway" that avoids "a new tax burden." Bonds still get paid back from somewhere, though. Add this to the Peak on the Parkway TIF (adoption Dec. 2) and the Nexus TIF application, and the city is lining up three ways to put public money behind private downtown projects. The question for each one is who pays, and over what timeline.

**First 2027 bill takes aim at Flock cameras.** Rep. Tom Gann (R-Inola) filed HB 1001, the "Oklahoma Freedom of Movement and Government Surveillance Prohibition Act." It would bar state and local agencies from using automated license-plate readers or paying a private company to track vehicles for them. The bill text is dated Nov. 17, and first reading is set for Feb. 1 (Oklahoma Legislature). Toll collection on the turnpikes is exempt. According to OK Politics Today, the bill would also end the camera-based uninsured-motorist program, and the Oklahoman reports an exception for use under a judge's particularized warrant. Oklahoma City's council voted 5–3 in August to keep its 90 cameras. This is a Republican going after a tool police and many Republican-run cities like, and nothing has been voted on yet. It's also a bigger fight than this week's Tulsa police drone pilot.

**PSO bills dip in December despite the rate hike.** The Corporation Commission's Nov. 5 order raised base rates 3.55% for residential customers, but PSO says rider changes will lower the average residential bill by about $5.80 starting next month. Industrial customers get refunds of this summer's interim overcharges, and households don't (Tulsa Flyer). Riders shift timing. They don't shrink the cost, so this is a one-month break and not a cheaper utility. The new large-load tariff for data centers is the part that decides who carries the next wave of capacity costs.

**Stitt v. Drummond, the sequel.** Gov. Stitt is personally asking for about $56,000 in legal fees and sanctions after AG Drummond dropped his lawsuit over the "wokest RINO" campaign ad. Stitt's attorney says the request is meant to discourage suing and then dismissing at the last minute (KTUL). It's a private dispute between two officials and costs taxpayers nothing either way.

**National and foreign.**

**Gulf shipping: one tanker strike is now independently confirmed.** *Confirmed (UKMTO, the Royal Navy's maritime trade office):* a tanker was hit by an unknown projectile about eight nautical miles north of Khasab, Oman, near the Strait of Hormuz, with a fire on board and no casualties. *Claimed by Iran:* the army says two tankers hit mines east of the strait after entering "unauthorized" routes with their transponders off, and the IRGC claims another hit a mine near Khasab. None of the ships is named, and no one has verified the mines. Separately, the Houthis told workers to evacuate Jeddah and other Saudi Red Sea ports after a Saudi-led coalition strike on Hodeidah, and they claim hits on Jazan port and a power station (Reuters, Al Jazeera). Iran gains if it can set the rules for which lanes ships may use. So far, traders mostly don't believe the threat: WTI settled at $83.98 (+$0.77) and Brent at $92.57 (+$0.59), but crude still fell for a third straight week (EnergyNow), and Hormuz traffic is reported back near prewar levels.

**Security watch.**

**NSA network: back up, and the cause is on the record.** *Confirmed (NSA spokesperson to The Record, Friday):* the agency is "now operating under normal conditions," though some facilities remain on delayed "Code Orange" openings for nonessential staff. DNI Clayton has publicly tied the three-day NSANet outage to PHP patches that kept systems from coming back up. That settles Wednesday's dispute: CBS called the network up too early, and NBC was right that it was still down Thursday. **Why it matters:** a routine patch took the country's main classified network down for three days. That's a resilience problem, not a hack, and nobody has said yet who signed off on the patch plan. Thread closed.

**FAA: the promised root cause didn't arrive this week.** Administrator Bedford said Thursday that Secretary Duffy would announce the cause this week. As of Friday evening, nothing has been released. What's known hasn't changed: engineers replicated the overload from a newly installed Juniper router at the El Dorado, Ark., site, and they still can't say whether it was installer error or a bad router. Meanwhile Duffy keeps blaming contractor L3Harris (AP). Blaming before the cause is announced is a political choice, not a finding.

Microsoft 365: the final post-incident report was due Friday, but it goes to customer admin portals and nothing public has surfaced yet. Nvidia: nothing new since California's subpoena.

**Economy and markets.**

**The AI trade breaks.** The S&P 500 fell 3.7% to 6,910 and is now in a correction, more than 10% below its Oct. 6 high. That's seven straight down days, the longest streak since 2020, and its worst week since April 2025. The Nasdaq fell 5.3% and is now negative for the year, and the Dow lost 1.9% to 48,147 (AP). Chips led the selling: Nvidia, Micron, Broadcom, AMD. AI-buildout names like Corning and Bloom Energy fell too, and Japan and Korea had each dropped more than 5% overnight. Anthropic fell about 9% on its second day to roughly $89, only a few dollars above its $83 IPO price. The early backers who sold into Thursday's $111 open got the best price of anyone. The market is repricing how much of the AI capex boom will ever earn a return. Remember that public subsidies and grid commitments were sized to the old story.

**The Fed is talking hikes into a falling market.** Governor Lisa Cook said Friday she doubts September's quarter-point increase "will be enough," and she warned that waiting risks "additional and larger rate increases" later (Fed speech text). Governor Waller has argued for a half point. Markets price a December quarter-point hike at roughly 90% or more. Chair Warsh has avoided signaling a direction while his colleagues fill the silence (CNBC). The 10-year eased to about 4.94% as money ran to safety, but real yields near 2.9% kept pushing gold down, to about $3,515. Plain version: the Fed is choosing to fight inflation over propping up asset prices, at least for now. Whether it holds that line through a stock correction is the test.

**Bitcoin.** It fell under $60,000 to about $59,500, its third straight daily drop, moving with tech stocks and not as a safe haven. No policy or protocol move.
