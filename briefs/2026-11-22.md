**Tulsa and Oklahoma.**

**Data center ban likely runs to March.** Tulsa's moratorium on new data center construction expires at the end of December. Councilor Laura Bellis has asked the council to extend it through March 31, and the planning office says it needs that time to turn its zoning recommendations into law. The extension is expected to pass this week. A public hearing is set for Dec. 2, and zoning changes could come up for a vote as early as January. Broken Arrow extended its own ban last week. Tulsa already has 18 data centers, mostly downtown, along Highway 75 and along the BA Expressway (Public Radio Tulsa). What matters isn't the pause. It's what the final rules say about noise, water, and who pays for the power lines and substations these sites need. Those costs connect to the large-load tariff regulators just created in the PSO rate case.

**Mazzei fills in the economic team.** Governor-elect Mike Mazzei named Commerce Department CEO John Budd as his chief operating officer. He also named working groups on agriculture (former Sen. Mike Schulz) and jobs and commerce (former Sen. Gary Stanislawski), plus a transition team that includes Choctaw Nation Chief Gary Batton (OKCFox). The goals he restated are ending the state income tax, abolishing property taxes for seniors and veterans, and limiting foreign land ownership. He has promised that step one of the income-tax plan will reach the Legislature the first week of February (KOCO). Asked Sunday about cattlemen's complaints that ICE enforcement has disrupted beef plants and shipments, he offered no plan beyond a coming talk with Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin about "a legal, well-trained workforce" (KOCO). The industry's labor problem and the enforcement push are pulling against each other, and so far nobody has put a policy on paper.

**National and foreign.**

**Hormuz is close to shut for ordinary cargo.** *Confirmed (Joint Maritime Information Center, Update 116, Nov. 22):* seven tankers have been hit in the Strait of Hormuz since Nov. 16. That includes three outbound tankers hit by unknown projectiles near Khasab, Oman, on Nov. 20–21, now named as the Torkel and the Arina plus one ship whose name is withheld. No crew were killed. S&P tracking shows no cargo ship over 1,000 tons making a visible transit on Nov. 19–21. U.S.-assisted transits rose to about 38 a day, up from 27 the week before. *Claimed by Iran:* on Sunday, the IRGC navy said two more tankers hit mines north of Ghanem Island because they used "unauthorized routes." The JMIC says no authority has confirmed any of Iran's mine claims. Separately, UKMTO is looking into an incident "involving a tanker and military forces" in the Gulf of Oman (ABC). *Confirmed by Bahrain:* an Iranian missile-and-drone attack on Saturday set fire to a civilian industrial site in Sitra and injured two people, one critically (The National, Reuters). Iran says it was aiming at U.S. Patriot batteries, and Saudi Arabia, Kuwait and Qatar condemned the attack. Tehran wants to charge passage under its own rules, and Washington is answering with escorts instead of talks. Watch Monday's crude open.

**Security watch.**

**Nvidia: Treasury on the record.** *Confirmed (CBS transcript, Face the Nation):* Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent says the government is in touch with Nvidia's interim management, lead directors and lawyers, and wants to know "who was in charge of compliance" and whether knowledge of the China sales went "all the way to the top." He said Treasury and other departments have pushed to blacklist Megaspeed, the Singapore firm at the center of the case. That puts on record that Commerce, which controls the blacklist, still hasn't acted. He called last week's remote-access rule, which blocks restricted entities from renting chip capacity through countries like Singapore and Malaysia, possibly more important than the blacklist. He also defended Commerce's earlier approvals of H200 chips for Chinese companies that do business with the Chinese military, saying the line drawn on chip type is what matters. Those approvals, for about 880,000 Nvidia and AMD chips, were made public Nov. 19 by House Foreign Affairs Chairman Brian Mast. *Reported, still unconfirmed:* the SEC accounting inquiry. **Why it matters:** if the administration focuses blame on Nvidia's compliance staff, it avoids scrutiny of what its own Commerce Department licensed. The Dec. 1 House interview with Huang is the next test.

**Economy and markets.**

**Bessent blames the Fed and the polls.** Speaking on the same show, Bessent tied last week's tech sell-off to Democrats' gains and their position on slowing the AI buildout, and to "very hawkish" Fed remarks. He said "many people believe" the Fed would make a policy mistake if it over-tightens. That's the Treasury leaning on the central bank in public, and it's commentary, not a rate decision. He also couldn't explain President Trump's Friday line that data centers won't be allowed in other countries, with "tremendous penalties" for companies that try. He said he hadn't discussed it with the president. No rule, order or penalty has been published, so for now it's only words.
