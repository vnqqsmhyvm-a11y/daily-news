**Tulsa and Oklahoma.**

**Hotel tax: visitors pay it, but the real ask is $80 million in bonds.** On Nov. 3, Tulsa voters decide whether to raise the hotel guest tax from 5% to 9.9% starting Jan. 1, 2027, the first increase in more than 40 years. The council's own page says the tax raised $10.4 million in 2026 and the new rate would bring in an estimated $20.6 million. The first use is $80 million in revenue bonds for the BOK Center and Arvest Convention Center. Added to $48 million already on hand from reserves, the arena operator Oak View Group and Improve Our Tulsa 3, that pays for $128 million of work. City leaders showed arena concepts Monday: new seats, a center-hung scoreboard, renovated suites and an event-day restaurant. Starting in 2031, more of the money shifts to marketing and event recruiting.

The pitch is that residents don't pay, and mostly they don't, though a local booking a Tulsa hotel stay does. The city compares itself to Oklahoma City at 9.25% and Norman at 10%, so 9.9% isn't out of line. The question the public material doesn't answer is what happens if collections come in under the $20.6 million estimate while the bond payments are fixed, and who covers the gap. That risk deserves more attention than the renderings. (Tulsa City Council, primary; News On 6. Fact / reporting / analysis.)

**TPS votes tonight on closing 17 sites.** The board takes a single up-or-down vote Monday night on closing or consolidating 14 elementary schools, two middle schools and Tulsa MET starting in 2027–28. The district projects about $11.1 million in savings, $8.5 million of it in staffing, against a $12 million permanent shortfall. The six elementary groups alone cover about 4,000 students, and Tulsa World puts total reassignments above 5,000. TPS says every site on the list enrolls fewer than 400 students and uses less than 65% of its building, and that Oklahoma City runs nearly the same enrollment with 22 fewer schools. The board can also table or reject it. (News On 6; Tulsa World. Reporting.)

**The DA grand jury petition is gathering signatures, and Kunzweiler is fighting it in court.** A citizens' group called Grand Jury Justice won a judge's approval in late September to circulate a petition for a grand jury on 11 areas. Those include specific case decisions, legal advice to county commissioners, conflicts of interest, records access, and whether county resources were used in his 2026 campaign. It needs 5,000 valid Tulsa County signatures within 45 days. District Attorney Steve Kunzweiler, reelected to a fourth term with nearly 60% in June's Republican primary, says most of it was aired in the campaign. The judge who approved the petition later recused, and Kunzweiler's lawyers have asked the new judge to vacate the approval. The petitioners' lawyer, Shar Agosto, is a longtime friend of his primary opponent, Colleen McCarty, who denies any role.

Oklahoma is one of about six states that let citizens do this. A DA can't be impeached here, but a grand jury can indict for removal. The last two tries, against the Payne County DA this year and Oklahoma Human Services in 2024, didn't get enough signatures. These are allegations, not findings. (Oklahoma Watch; Tulsa World. Reporting.)

**Plate cameras.** TPD's license-plate-reader session at Tulsa Tech runs 6 to 7 p.m. tonight, with questions submitted in advance only. The numbers worth listening for are how many searches officers run, how much data leaves TPD, and how long it's kept.

**National and foreign.**

**Russia: the bank-sanctions deadline passed under the waiver, and the diesel deal hasn't reached the pump.** The Graham Act's 30-day deadline for sanctions on Sberbank, VTB, Gazprombank and other Russian state banks fell this weekend. Friday's executive order had already waived most of those provisions, so nothing took effect. Secretary of State Marco Rubio says the President will take up to 60 days to assess the sanctions' effect on the world economy. The other half of the trade is diesel. Reuters reports Russian exports starting at about 2.3 million barrels this month and rising to about 7.6 million a month, under a Treasury license that runs through April 7. Neither government has published the deal's terms. The law firm Herbert Smith Freehills Kramer notes the license doesn't address the 2022 statute banning Russian oil imports, which can be lifted only through a process that includes a presidential certification and a 90-day congressional review. So any U.S. imports rest on contestable legal ground.

The pump says the deal hasn't landed. AAA had Tulsa diesel at $5.97 Monday, up from $5.84 a week ago and just under the metro record of $5.99 set Sept. 22. Analysts say Ukrainian drone strikes on Russian refineries limit how much Russia actually has to sell. The incentive is pump prices two weeks before the midterms. The Senate voted 86–11 for mandatory sanctions, then handed the White House the waiver it is now using. (Treasury license text, primary; Reuters; Herbert Smith Freehills Kramer; AAA, primary. Fact / reporting / analysis.)

**Hormuz: a claimed mine strike, claimed Houthi hits on Saudi Arabia, and flows at less than half normal.** Iran's Revolutionary Guard says a tanker struck a mine and exploded in the southern strait Sunday. The ship isn't named, and the claim comes only from Iranian media. The Houthis say they hit Aramco's Yanbu facility, the Shaybah oilfield and two Saudi airports Monday. Riyadh hasn't confirmed it, and Reuters couldn't verify it. A separate Houthi attack on a Yemeni government training camp in Shabwa killed 53 people, Reuters reported. Kpler put Gulf crude flows at about 8.5 million barrels a day last week, with at least 10 confirmed incidents involving commercial ships. Brent settled at $109.55, down 31 cents. Expiring November WTI rose 2.2% to $103.66 as refiners bid for domestic barrels. (Reuters; DTN. Claims are claims.)

**Supreme Court: two liberty signals on Monday's order list.** The Court agreed to hear the government's appeal in *DOJ v. McNutt*, where the Fifth Circuit struck down the federal ban on distilling spirits at home. The question is whether a ban on *where* you distill is "necessary and proper" to collecting the liquor tax, a real test of how far the taxing power reaches. Separately, the Court declined a challenge to Chicago's practice of booting, impounding and selling any car owned by someone with two tickets unpaid for over a year, even a car that never got a ticket. One plaintiff's car was sold for scrap for $273 over $524 in tickets. Justice Sotomayor, joined by Justice Gorsuch, wrote that features of the city code "strongly suggest" the purpose "is to collect a debt, not enforce public safety laws," which would make it a possible taking. A denial isn't a ruling, but two justices from opposite wings flagged forfeiture for revenue. (Supreme Court order list and statement, primary; Fifth Circuit opinion; CBS Chicago. Fact.)

**Economics and geopolitics.**

**The 10-year is at 5.15%, and the Fed is expected to sit still.** The 10-year Treasury yield rose about 4 basis points Monday to 5.15%, with the 30-year at 5.48% and the 2-year at 4.77%. That follows September wholesale prices up 6.3% from a year ago and consumer prices up 3.7%. Futures now put better than 80% odds on no change at the Oct. 27–28 Fed meeting. A pause with inflation running this hot means the bond market is doing the tightening, and the long end is where the bill for federal borrowing shows up. The 30-year mortgage averaged 7.40% last week. September housing starts and permits come out Tuesday. Gold is about $4,158, little changed. (CNBC; CME FedWatch via CNBC; BLS; Freddie Mac via FRED. Fact / analysis.)

Bitcoin is about $82,000, flat, with no policy or protocol news.
