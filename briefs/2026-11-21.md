**Tulsa and Oklahoma.**

**Mazzei names his money watchdogs.** Governor-elect Mike Mazzei picked Chris Pembrook, the University of Oklahoma's chief audit executive and a seven-year veteran of the State Auditor's office, as state CFO. Former state Sen. Anthony Sykes will be general counsel and draft the executive orders aimed at waste, fraud and abuse (OK Politics Today). For now these are hires and promises. The test is what the first orders actually require agencies to disclose or cut, and whether any of it survives the Legislature's budget writers. In the Legislature, House Republicans again backed Kyle Hilbert for speaker and Senate Republicans backed Lonnie Paxton as pro tem. Both votes become formal Jan. 5, and Republicans hold 75–26 in the House and 40–8 in the Senate (KGOU).

**GM pays Oklahoma $4.18 million over driver data.** AG Gentner Drummond says GM sold Smart Driver data, meaning driving behavior and location, to data brokers who resold it to insurers. Insurers could then use it to price premiums, even though GM told drivers it wouldn't share their data without consent. The state's share comes from a 37-state settlement worth more than $200 million. According to the AG, dealership staff were paid bonuses for OnStar sign-ups and sometimes accepted the terms for the customer, and the OnStar app told more than 370,000 enrolled drivers they weren't enrolled (Oklahoma AG release). This is a consumer-protection settlement, not a court finding. If you've owned a 2016–2024 GM vehicle with OnStar, your insurance rate may have been priced on data you never knowingly gave.

**Illinois River poultry deal rejected again.** On Wednesday, federal Judge Gregory Frizzell turned down the proposed roughly $44 million settlement in the state's poultry-litter pollution case, calling it inadequate. He cited thin funding detail, weak oversight, no soil-phosphorus limits, and a seven-year term too short for the problem (Arkansas Democrat-Gazette, Oklahoman). The case has run nearly two decades, and the river and Lake Tenkiller still lack a remedy.

**National and foreign.**

**Two more tankers hit at Hormuz.** *Confirmed (UKMTO, the Royal Navy's maritime trade office):* on Saturday one outbound tanker was hit in the stern by an unidentified projectile about 11 nautical miles north of Khasab, Oman. Another was hit in the engine room 17 miles east of Khasab. It isn't clear whether that was the same ship, and no casualties were reported. *Claimed by Iran:* the navy says a tanker caught fire after hitting a mine in a "restricted area" east of the strait. Nobody has independently verified that or matched it to the UKMTO reports (CNBC/Reuters). This makes three straight days of strikes. Tehran's incentive is to make its own shipping rules the price of passage and to squeeze Washington. Markets were closed Saturday, so Monday's crude open will show whether traders still shrug this off.

**Security watch.**

**Microsoft 365: final report out, thread closed.** *Confirmed (Microsoft final post-incident report, dated Nov. 20):* the Nov. 12 outage came from a latent bug in a Windows build used only on Exchange Online's gateway servers. The July update that introduced it changed how those servers set up incoming connections. Under heavy load, tasks jammed until the servers ran out of CPU and memory. Three things set it off: about 14% of capacity was down for planned maintenance (the preliminary report had called it "an unrelated issue"), a Teams routing glitch in US East pushed all Teams traffic worldwide onto the email gateways, and the U.S. workday hit its peak. Failover then moved the load onto healthy servers that had the same bug, so within about two hours every region was affected. Microsoft says it was "not caused by a cyberattack," "not caused by AI tooling," and no business mail was lost. It lists 13 fixes, including regional isolation, separating email from Teams traffic, and lab hardware that matches production. Some are due by year-end and others by March, and several have no date (GeekWire; report text). **Why it matters:** the safety system built to contain failures is what spread this one worldwide, and Microsoft's own lab missed the bug because it tested on weaker machines. Microsoft is grading its own work here, and no regulator is checking it.

**Nvidia: SEC probe reported, not confirmed.** *Reported (New York Times, following the Wall Street Journal, both citing people briefed):* the SEC has an early-stage inquiry into Nvidia's accounting. One question is last year's change to geographic revenue reporting, from billing address to customer headquarters, which made Singapore drop out of the breakdown even though Nvidia does heavy business there. That matters because of Megaspeed, the Singapore firm under U.S. investigation for shipping restricted chips to China. The SEC has not confirmed anything, and these inquiries often end without charges. *Confirmed on the record:* Chairman Harvey Jones says the board will not reinstate Jensen Huang, even after President Trump said Friday he'd "love to see Jensen be given a second chance." Three House committees have a transcribed interview with Huang set for Dec. 1 (AFP, Yahoo Finance). Nvidia shares fell 14% Friday and more than 20% on the week.

FAA: still no official root cause for the Nov. 13 ground stop, two days after the promised "this week."

**Economy and markets.**

**Bitcoin.** It dropped to $55,130 early Saturday, its lowest since September 2024, then bounced about 9% to roughly $60,300 (CoinDesk). About $1.8 billion in leveraged positions were liquidated, most of them bets on higher prices, and U.S. spot ETFs saw about $1.1 billion in net outflows on the week. This was forced selling clearing out, not a policy or protocol change. It is still trading like a tech stock, so Monday's Nasdaq open matters more than anything said about bitcoin itself.
