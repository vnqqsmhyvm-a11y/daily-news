**Tulsa and Oklahoma.**

**TPS voted 6–1 to close 17 schools.** After a four-and-a-half-hour meeting with 30 public speakers, the board approved the superintendent's plan as written Monday night. Fourteen elementary schools, two middle schools and Tulsa MET close at the end of this school year, and about 4,000 students move to receiving schools in 2027–28. The district says the closures save $11.1 million, $8.5 million of it staffing and $2.6 million operations. The same motion orders the superintendent to find another $1.5 million in cuts, against a $12 million permanent shortfall. TPS also says no certified teachers lose their jobs, which leaves open where the $8.5 million in staffing savings actually comes from; the district hasn't itemized it. Several buildings, including Bunche, have no plan yet, and empty buildings still cost money to keep up or sell. (TPS agenda and board message, primary; StateImpact Oklahoma via KOSU; Tulsa World. Fact.)

**The sheriff says he'll keep running plate-reader searches after a federal judge called them mass surveillance.** On Oct. 1, U.S. District Judge Sara Hill threw out evidence in *U.S. v. Kyle*. A Tulsa County deputy had pulled about a month of a rental car's travel history from Flock and a second system, more than 50 sightings across several states. The order says he followed the car "for no apparent reason other than" its California plate. He then stopped it for following too closely and held the driver for a dog sniff, which turned up what the sheriff says was 91 pounds of meth. Hill ruled the database search was itself a warrantless Fourth Amendment search, and that the stop was stretched without reasonable suspicion.

Sheriff Vic Regalado says the deputy followed policy, an appeal to the Tenth Circuit is expected, and deputies will keep using the system "until there has been a decision probably by the high courts." He's within his rights there. The order itself admits it breaks with nearly every court that has ruled so far, including three other Oklahoma federal courts, and it binds only that case. But the fact pattern is the problem: a search with no suspicion beyond an out-of-state plate is exactly the discretionary use the department says its audits are meant to catch, and the sheriff calls it within policy. Tulsa Police say the city has 106 Flock cameras and answered nearly 80 pre-submitted questions at Monday's session. The next one is Oct. 26 at Tulsa Tech's Memorial campus. (Court order, primary; News On 6; KJRH. Fact / analysis.)

**Tulsa County cut 32 road jobs on the spot.** Commissioners dissolved the Highway Construction Division Monday, eliminating 32 of its 40 positions effective immediately, with pay through Nov. 20. The county says the work moves to each commissioner's district maintenance yard, other departments and outside contractors, and calls it more cost-efficient. It hasn't published the dollar comparison. That's the number to ask for: what the county paid in-house versus what the vendors will charge. (News On 6. Reporting.)

**Osage Nation police searched two homeschool sites.** On Oct. 13, Osage Nation police, with Pawhuska police helping, served state-court warrants on a downtown Pawhuska building and a private home where a new homeschool program teaches former students of the Nation's language-immersion school, which the principal chief closed in August. The warrants list larceny, possession of stolen property, conspiracy and contributing to delinquency, and they cover bookshelves, library books, a bench, "any and all" backpacks and phones. The homeowner says officers took her phone and told her to unlock it by 3 p.m. or it would go to the FBI. The former superintendent says most of the books were grant-funded and meant to go home with families. No charges have been filed, and Osage legislators are now asking why the warrants reached children's backpacks. (Osage News, the Nation's paper; Tulsa World. Allegations, not findings.)

**National and foreign.**

**Oil touched a five-month high and fell back. Diesel didn't.** Brent hit $111.99 Tuesday before settling at $108.79, down 76 cents. Expiring November WTI ran as high as $110.48 and settled at $101.79. The UK's maritime security office counts 30 attacks in and around Hormuz this month, and Kpler confirms 18 ship strikes in two weeks. Attacks have now reached the Gulf of Oman, where Saudi Arabia does ship-to-ship transfers to get around the strait. Saudi Arabia hasn't confirmed the Houthi claims of strikes on Yanbu and Shaybah, but it postponed a major economic forum. Iran's president met Pakistan's army chief, who brokered June's short-lived interim deal. Deutsche Bank calls a U.S.–Iran agreement before the midterms "increasingly difficult."

On Russia, the first diesel cargoes since the sanctions waiver are headed to Turkey and Brazil, according to ship tracking. That's world supply, not American tanks. Energy Secretary Chris Wright says Washington is pushing refiners to run harder and shuttered refineries to reopen. Those are words until a refinery actually restarts. Meanwhile, AAA has Tulsa diesel at $5.98, a penny under the Sept. 22 record and up from $3.14 a year ago. (DTN; Reuters; Dow Jones Market Data; AAA, primary. Ship-strike claims are claims.)

**Economics and geopolitics.**

**Housing starts fell to a near-two-year low.** Census says September starts ran at a 1.221 million annual rate, down 5.2% from August and 7.4% from a year ago. Only May has been lower in the last 25 months. Apartments drove the drop: starts in buildings with five or more units are down 26% from a year ago, while single-family starts are still up 2.6%. Permits were flat at 1.40 million. Census's own margin of error on the monthly change is plus or minus 11.8%, so one month isn't a trend. The direction is what you'd expect with the 30-year mortgage at 7.40% and builders paying more for materials and labor. A falling pipeline today means tighter supply later, which works against the price relief buyers keep being promised. (Census, primary; Construction Metrics; Freddie Mac via FRED. Fact / analysis.)

**Rates.** The 10-year eased to about 5.12% on the housing miss, and the 2-year rose to 4.79%. Futures put 82% odds on no change at next week's Fed meeting, up from 42% a month ago. PCE inflation, the Fed's preferred gauge, comes out next week. Gold slipped about half a percent to roughly $4,121. Bitcoin is about $82,450, little changed, with no policy or protocol news. (CNBC; CME FedWatch via CNBC; Babypips market wrap. Fact.)
