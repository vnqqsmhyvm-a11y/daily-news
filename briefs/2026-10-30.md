**Tulsa and Oklahoma.** Early voting ends Saturday, 8 a.m. to 2 p.m.; Election Day is Tuesday, polls 7 to 7. Two state questions are on every ballot, SQ 845 and SQ 847, plus retention votes on nine judges (State Election Board). Early turnout is running ahead of the last midterm, with the biggest gains among Democrats and independents (KFOR). Clocks fall back an hour early Sunday morning.

The Senate seat matters sooner than you might think. Attorney General Gentner Drummond's opinion says Tuesday's winner of the open U.S. Senate seat can take office as soon as the State Election Board certifies the result, filling the rest of the current term, rather than waiting until January 3. Appointed Sen. Alan Armstrong's service ends when the winner is sworn in ([AG Opinion 2026-18](https://oklahoma.gov/oag/opinions/opinions/ag-opinions/2026/18.html); Oklahoma Voice). That means a lame-duck Senate vote or two could be cast by the new senator.


The Inola smelter picked up formal tribal opposition. The Cherokee Nation's Rules Committee unanimously passed a resolution opposing the $4 billion Emirates Global Aluminum and Century Aluminum project, backing the Muscogee Nation, which opposed it in July. It now goes to the full Tribal Council (KOSU). A resident petition to put the project to a vote next spring is being challenged by the City of Tulsa–Rogers County Port Authority. Follow the money: the state, the governor, and the White House back it; the people who live next to it, and now both neighboring tribes, want a say first.

Tulsa is writing its data center rules before the first big one shows up. The Planning Office draft would allow hyperscale data centers by right only in moderate and heavy industrial zones, with a proposed 1,500-foot setback from homes and farmland and a 100-foot landscaping buffer (Tulsa World [paywalled, headline only]; Hoodline summary). The Planning Commission takes it up Wednesday, Nov. 4, as item ZCA-41 ([Tulsa Planning Office agenda](https://tulsaplanning.org/agendas/november-4-2026-planning-commission-work-session-agenda/)). Oklahoma City approved its first hyperscale center this week. Separately, the state's groundwater law, SB 259, takes effect Sunday and bars new data-center well permits for evaporative cooling. Zoning decides where these go; the bigger question for ratepayers is still who pays for the power lines, which is a Corporation Commission fight, not a zoning one.

Safe Move Tulsa, the city's homelessness push, has rehoused 820 people against a goal of 1,300 by the end of 2026 (The Oklahoma Eagle). The city hasn't put the cost per person housed next to that number; that's the figure to ask for.

An unsealed State Farm document in an Oklahoma County lawsuit shows an internal summary warning executives that its wind and hail claim handling looked like it was "intentionally reducing" payouts (The Oklahoman). It's one side's exhibit in an ongoing case, not a verdict. But if you've had a hail claim denied, it's worth knowing.

**Faith, order, money, power.** The Strait of Hormuz had its worst month of the war. The British navy's maritime monitor counted just over 30 attacks on ships in October, double September and a record for the war. On Friday a tanker was hit by limpet mines attached from a small boat, the first such attack of the war, and Oman evacuated 25 crew from a burning Panama-flagged tanker. Iran's Revolutionary Guard claims several more tankers hit mines on the US-backed southern route, which it calls illegal; those claims aren't verified. US Central Command's commander repeated that Iran's navy is "at the bottom of the sea" and the southern lanes are open (CBS, AFP, UKMTO). Both claims can't be fully true at once, and the ships keep getting hit.

Diplomacy is moving in the same days. Qatari mediators went back to Tehran with edits to a draft deal, and a CBS source says there are signs of progress; Iran's answer is expected this weekend. Tehran still wants the port blockade lifted and sanctions eased first, while Washington wants the nuclear program on the table from the start. Trump told a Kansas City radio station the war is "going to get wrapped up soon," and said Vance's letter to Iran is only about medical supplies, which is not how Iranian media or US outlets have described it. Words point to a deal; the deeds on the water point the other way. The decision still looks timed for after Tuesday.

**Economics in plain language.** A correction in emphasis from last night: the monthly inflation number came out Thursday too, and it's worse than the quarterly figure suggested. The PCE price index rose 0.5% in September and 3.6% from a year ago; core, without food and energy, was 3.1%. Gasoline and other energy goods jumped 9.2% in the month. Real after-tax income fell 0.3%, inflation-adjusted spending was flat, and the savings rate is 3.8% (BEA). Put plainly, people are spending more dollars to buy the same stuff, and their paychecks didn't keep up. That's the backdrop for the Fed's two hike votes.

The AI trade had its worst week in a while. The Nasdaq fell 2.9% Friday and 5.3% for October, its worst month since March 2025; the S&P 500 lost 1.8% (Investopedia). Intel fell about 29% after a weak fourth-quarter outlook and a foundry loss of about $1.9 billion in the quarter; it had been priced at more than 50 times 2027 earnings. Amazon missed estimates and reported falling free cash flow because of AI spending. After Alphabet's 13% drop Thursday, the market is asking the same question of each giant: when does the AI buildout pay for itself.

Oil went the other way. December WTI settled at $104.12, up $2.12, and Brent's January contract at $113.43; the expiring December Brent hit $117.64. Brent rose about 14% in October and WTI about 15% (Reuters via CNA). ExxonMobil's third-quarter profit doubled to $15.4 billion (CBS/AFP). OPEC+ meets Sunday and is expected to hold its targets. Gold futures fell 3.7% to about $3,880, the lowest since September 2025,, down more than a quarter from January's high above $5,300, as markets price a possible Fed hike. The 10-year Treasury yield sits near 4.93%.

**Bitcoin.** Bitcoin fell from near $82,000 Thursday to about $76,500 after Binance, the largest exchange, lost about $6.3 billion in what's being called the biggest crypto theft on record. Blockchain analysts Chainalysis and TRM attribute it with high confidence to North Korea's Lazarus group, and the Secret Service is helping trace funds. Binance froze withdrawals, says losses will be covered, and expects to reopen withdrawals within about two days (AP; BBC; Chainalysis). This was a failure of a company holding other people's coins, not of the Bitcoin protocol. The old rule held again: coins on an exchange are an IOU until you can withdraw them.
