**Tulsa and Oklahoma.** Election Day is Tuesday; polls are open 7 a.m. to 7 p.m., and anyone in line at 7 gets to vote (State Election Board). Mailed absentee ballots have to reach the county election board by 5 p.m. Monday; hand-delivered ones by 7 p.m. Tuesday. Check your polling place on the OK Voter Portal, since you have to vote at your assigned precinct. Monday's forecast calls for strong storms in eastern Oklahoma late afternoon into the evening, with damaging wind and an isolated tornado risk, clearing by Tuesday (National Weather Service, via Hoodline).

Final early-voting numbers are in. Oklahomans cast 186,488 in-person early ballots Wednesday through Saturday, up 41% from 2022. Republicans cast more ballots than four years ago but their share fell from about 55% to 48%; Democrats rose from about 35% to 38% and independents from about 10% to 13%. The shift was concentrated in six metro counties; across the other 71, Republicans were still about 58% (Oklahoma Politics Today, from State Election Board reports). Registration is not a vote, and some people may simply have voted earlier than usual. What it does show is that Munson needs a much bigger metro margin than Hofmeister got in 2022, and the early electorate is at least shaped the way she'd need it to be.

On the money side of the governor's race: Mazzei has spent nearly $15 million, mostly from personal loans, against about $2.9 million for Munson, and the Republican and Democratic governors' associations have put in $3 million and $500,000 (The Oklahoman). Self-funding that size for a job that pays a fraction of it is worth weighing either way: few donors to answer to, but a large personal bet.

Three things on your ballot besides candidates:

- **SQ 847** lowers how fast assessed property values can rise each year starting in 2027: from 3% to 1.75% for homesteads and farmland, and from 5% to 4% for everything else. It doesn't cut tax rates. It slows how fast your bill can grow, which also slows how fast school and county revenue grows; that is why OK Policy is campaigning against it.
- **SQ 845** rewrites the Judicial Nominating Commission, the body that screens state judges for the governor. Per the filed ballot title: the governor and the state bar each appoint six members, any number of commissioners can be lawyers, the ban on family members practicing law goes away, party-balance limits come off the governor's and at-large picks, and commissioners can serve back-to-back terms. Today the governor's appointees can't be lawyers, so the plain effect is to let the legal profession hold more of the seats. No fiscal impact.
- **Tulsa Proposition 1** raises the hotel and short-term-rental tax from 5% to 9.9% on Jan. 1, 2027. The city projects revenue going from $10.4 million to about $20.6 million a year, backing $80 million in revenue bonds (Tulsa City Council). That projection is the city's own. Residents pay it too, on any stay under 30 days.

Outside the ballot, the Tulsa Community Foundation launched a $1 million matching drive for the new city animal shelter at 36th Street North, a $13.8 million voter-approved bond project due to open in early 2027 (FOX23). A private donor put up $1 million to start a permanent endowment. That is private money taking on running costs the bond didn't cover.

**Faith, order, money, power.** Iranian negotiators spent a third day in Doha with Qatari mediators. Foreign Minister Araghchi says the "two-day plan" to reopen the Strait of Hormuz is "progressing" but there's no deal and no direct talks with Americans (CNBC/Reuters; Iran International). Tehran's terms are unchanged: lift the US naval blockade, release frozen funds including about $6 billion held in Qatar, allow oil sales, and approve an Iranian-backed shipping route through the strait. Washington wants nuclear concessions first (Axios, via Iran International). Rubio said Saturday the president hasn't decided whether to resume strikes, and Trump says talks are "going fine" but he's "not in that much of a hurry." The UAE's Anwar Gargash said the Gulf states are "braced for the worst" (Guardian). The incentive is plain on both sides: Trump promised no new attacks before Tuesday, and Iran is trying to get money and its shipping route before that promise runs out. Al Araby's report last week of a "breakthrough this week" did not happen.

**Economics in plain language.** OPEC+ did what it signaled: the seven core members kept December production targets at September's level, about 31 million barrels a day, with Saudi Arabia at 10.478 million (OPEC statement). It changes little in practice, because most of them are pumping below target anyway with the strait shut. The real decision is Nov. 29, when the full 21-country group sets 2027 quotas. Brent settled near $117.64 on Friday, a six-month high and its fourth straight monthly gain (The National). October had at least 35 attacks on commercial ships in and around the strait, the most of the war.

The week ahead: ISM manufacturing Monday, ISM services Wednesday, and the October jobs report Friday, where forecasters expect about 65,000 jobs and 4.2% unemployment (TD Economics calendar). The Fed held at 3.75% to 4% last week with two members voting for a hike. A hot jobs number plus $117 oil makes a December hike more likely, and that is a forecast, not a fact.

**Bitcoin.** Binance reopened withdrawals Sunday as promised, starting with bitcoin and adding 15 networks by early afternoon UTC. It says it has now covered about 95% of the $6.3 billion stolen, up from 90%, with the rest in smaller, less liquid tokens (Binance announcement). Customers pulled about $4.65 billion and deposited about $1 billion in the first nine and a half hours, a net outflow of roughly 2% of the $171 billion Binance reports holding (Bitcoin.com News). That's a run that didn't happen, at least so far. The full cryptographic proof of reserves is still due Tuesday. Until then the backing is Binance's word plus a snapshot. Bitcoin was around $78,200 (CoinDesk).
