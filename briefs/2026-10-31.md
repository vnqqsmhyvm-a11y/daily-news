**Tulsa and Oklahoma.** Early voting closed at 2 p.m. today; Election Day is Tuesday, polls open 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. Through the first two days of early voting, 99,586 Oklahomans voted in person, up from 72,008 at the same point in 2022. In Tulsa County it was 12,805, up from 8,428, and registered Democrats outnumbered Republicans there, 6,344 to 4,573 (Tulsa World's table of State Election Board data [paywalled; numbers not yet checked against the Board's own reports]). Early voters skew older and more partisan than Election Day voters, so this tells you about enthusiasm, not the result.

Tulsa County is getting out of the road-building business. Commissioners voted to dissolve the county's Highway Construction division, cutting 32 jobs; workers stay on payroll through Nov. 20. The three district maintenance yards keep pothole, ditch, and snow work, and larger road and bridge jobs will go to private contractors (News On 6). Commissioner Stan Sallee says the structure dates to 1978 and rising equipment costs drove the change, and that it will save taxpayer money. That's the claim; the county hasn't published what the division cost against what contractors will charge, which is the number that decides whether this is thrift or just moving the bill. Unincorporated Tulsa County covers roughly 680 road miles and 180 bridges. Statewide, the Transportation secretary says Oklahoma is about $255 million a year short just to keep roads and bridges where they are (Journal Record, via Hoodline).

More than 200 state laws take effect tomorrow (Oklahoma Voice). The ones with teeth: marriage age is now 18 with no exceptions, after passing the House by a single vote; some domestic-violence defendants out on bond must wear a GPS tracker their victim can follow; the governor loses the power to pick the Tourism Department director after the vendor fraud scandal, which goes back to the commission; and the Turnpike Authority can start scouting a new Arkansas River bridge near Bixby, with local governments on the hook for the roads leading to it. The groundwater rule that blocks new data-center well permits for evaporative cooling also starts tomorrow.

**Faith, order, money, power.** Iran answered the latest Qatari push, and the answer is mostly the same list with a shorter clock. Foreign Minister Araghchi told Al Jazeera that Tehran could reopen the Strait of Hormuz within two days, down from the five Qatar proposed, if Washington lifts the sanctions imposed since the June memorandum collapsed, restores oil waivers, frees frozen funds including about $6 billion in Qatar, reduces US forces in the region, and recognizes Iran's preferred shipping routes while closing the others. He called the Vance letter "a positive step" but said there's no need to meet the Americans yet. Iranian negotiators were in Doha today (Reuters via Al-Monitor). Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei, who still hasn't appeared in public, ordered the military to answer any attack immediately and "without geographic limits" (AP). Read it plainly: Iran is offering speed, not concessions, and the closure of the US-backed southern route is the price. The incentive on both sides is to look reasonable before Tuesday while keeping the strike option alive after it.

**Economics in plain language.** OPEC+ meets tomorrow, and its seven core members have agreed in principle to keep December output targets unchanged, according to Reuters reporting carried by Argaam and Investing.com. With Brent above $110 and the strait still contested, holding steady means the cartel isn't stepping in to cool prices. Nothing else on the macro calendar moved on a Saturday.

**Bitcoin.** Binance says it has covered about 90% of the $6.3 billion stolen from its hot wallets, using its corporate treasury, part of its futures insurance fund, and its user-protection fund, and still plans to reopen withdrawals tomorrow. Bitcoin, ether, and eight other major tokens are fully backed, it says; the gap is in smaller, less liquid coins. The FBI said late today that North Korea was behind it (CNBC; CoinDesk). Two cautions. "Covered" means Binance replaced the coins from its own pocket, not that it got them back. And the full cryptographic proof that would let customers verify their balances isn't due until Tuesday, so until then the backing is Binance's word plus a snapshot. Bitcoin sat near $77,400 today, roughly flat.
