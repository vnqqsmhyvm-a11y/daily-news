**Tulsa and Oklahoma.**

**A federal appeals court upheld Oklahoma's environmental authority across Indian Country.** On Oct. 16 a Tenth Circuit panel (Tymkovich writing, with Bacharach and Federico) denied the Pawnee Nation's challenge to EPA's May 2025 approval letting the state run more than two dozen federal environmental programs on tribal land in eastern Oklahoma. The hook is a 2005 "Midnight Rider," a provision slipped into a highway bill without debate, which lay unused for 15 years until *McGirt* made most of eastern Oklahoma Indian Country. The court held the provision never expired, that EPA had to approve the request once it met the statute, and that EPA couldn't attach the tribal-consultation conditions it had added in January 2025 and then dropped in May. Gov. Stitt called it a victory for "the rule of law." The deed here is Congress's, not the governor's: a rider nobody debated is now settled law for permits across most of eastern Oklahoma. Inola sits inside the Cherokee reservation, so the ruling likely bears on the smelter's state air permit too (our inference). The Pawnee can still ask for rehearing or go to the Supreme Court. (Tenth Circuit opinion, primary; governor's statement. Fact.)

**The Inola smelter is training workers for a plant it can't build yet.** Oklahoma Primary Aluminum, the EGA-led project, held a three-day workforce session at Tulsa Tech this week with Tulsa Tech, Northeast Tech, TCC, Rogers State, OSU and OU. CEO Jake Skelton says 1,000 direct jobs and groundbreaking next year "pending permitting." Rogers State plans to use a new $35 million STEM lab and start a three-year degree next fall. A downstream firm, U.S. Aluminum, says about 200 jobs. The 6,000 "indirect" jobs are a projection, not a hire. Earlier this month the company said its roughly $100,000 average pay figure includes overtime and cash benefits, and it hasn't given base wages. What hasn't moved: DEQ is still reviewing the air permit and has asked for more support for the emissions math, Inola's moratorium runs through April 7, 2027, and a citizen ban on primary smelting is on Inola's April 6 ballot. Tulsa Ports authorized a lawsuit against the town last week; we haven't found a filed complaint. Training talk is cheap until someone signs a power contract and a permit. (NewsOn6; company answers via North Atlanta Star; DEQ project page, primary. Reporting / analysis.)

**The charter board will try a third vote on the Jewish charter school Friday.** Ben Gamla failed 4–3 on Oct. 12, one short of the five votes state law requires for any final action. The board's lawyer now says a 4–3 vote was "no final action" either way, so it's back on Friday's noon agenda along with a possible appeal of the September federal ruling that a charter can't be denied just for being religious. Last week both the board chair and Attorney General Drummond said the second rejection was final. The founder told Tulsa Jewish leaders he expects 10 students in year one against an application projecting hundreds, and the loudest opposition has come from Oklahoma's Jewish community itself. The real question is constitutional: whether taxpayer money can fund a religious school run as a public charter. A vote that keeps getting re-held until it passes is a process story worth watching regardless of where you land on that. (Oklahoma Voice; board agenda, primary. Fact / reporting.)

**Election check.** Early voting in Tulsa County runs Oct. 28–31. Statewide, SQ 845 (Judicial Nominating Commission) and SQ 847 (property-tax assessment caps) are on the ballot; both were covered earlier this week. KOSU's preview of SQ 845 adds that Stitt's six JNC picks are three Republicans and three independents, which the measure's author cites as the reason to drop party limits. (KOSU; SQ 845 ballot title, primary.)

**National and foreign.**

**Putin agreed to restart U.S.-brokered talks with Ukraine.** After meeting envoy Steve Witkoff, the Kremlin says it's ready to resume three-way talks with Kyiv and Washington, as soon as next month. That's a statement, not a ceasefire. The market cares because a deal could free up Russian diesel; it took a couple of dollars out of crude intraday. (Kremlin readout, primary; AP; Reuters. Fact / speculation on outcome.)

**Oil ended flat after another $2 swing.** Brent settled at $108.82, up 3 cents, and WTI fell 67 cents to $97.15, after both ran up more than $2 when Iran's foreign minister said there was little reason to expect progress with the U.S. At least two more ships were reported struck overnight. EIA says U.S. crude stocks fell 3.1 million barrels last week, more than expected, but distillates, which include diesel, rose 1.7 million barrels when a draw was expected. One week isn't relief at the pump. (Reuters, citing EIA and Kpler. Fact.)
