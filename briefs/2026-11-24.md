**Tulsa and Oklahoma.**

**Stitt won't show the AG what he's paying outside lawyers.** The governor's office spent more than $3.1 million from February 2020 to June 2026 on private attorneys for fights with tribes and challenges to McGirt, according to state spending records obtained by The Frontier. At least $1.9 million of that came from the Tribal Gaming Compliance fund, which is paid for by tribal fees. State law requires executive-branch offices to give the Attorney General copies of outside-attorney contracts, plus the scope of work and estimated cost. The AG's office says Stitt's office sent nothing in 2020, 2021, 2024 or 2025. A 2025 Oklahoma Supreme Court ruling lets the governor hire his own lawyers without AG approval, and his office points to that. The ruling didn't erase the reporting requirement, though. Rep. Judd Strom, who wrote this year's spending-transparency law, says it now falls to a district attorney or a judge to force the issue. Stitt signed that law. Most of these contracts aren't in the new state spending database. Separately, Stitt and School Freedom Fund Oklahoma have asked an Osage County judge to make Drummond pay $263,000 in legal costs after Drummond dropped his defamation suit against them (OK Politics Today, from court filings).

**Agencies want $1.3 billion more, and Washington is part of the reason.** Fiscal 2028 budget requests from 71 of 75 agencies add up to about $1.3 billion, nearly 11% above this year's base, per the House Budget Transparency Portal (KOSU). Human Services is asking for the most, about $192 million. Of that, $78.9 million is for SNAP, because last year's federal H.R. 1 shifted more of the program's cost onto states. That includes $45 million set aside for a possible federal penalty. The state can avoid that penalty only by getting its SNAP payment-error rate under 6%. USDA has it at 11.04%. In the worst case, the state's share of benefit costs could reach $250 million by FY28. Washington booked the savings, and Oklahoma taxpayers get the bill. It lands as lawmakers and Gov.-elect Mazzei promise more income and property tax cuts.

**Mazzei adds a general counsel.** Former Sen. Anthony Sykes, author of the English-official-language state question and recently the AG's first public access counselor, will be the governor's general counsel (Journal Record). New Secretary of State Eric Roberts lost his House seat on Nov. 3.

**State law now puts Tulsa and OKC on the hook for enforcement.** The Oklahoma Safe Neighborhoods Act took effect Nov. 1. It lets property owners file claims against the two cities if they fail to enforce laws on public camping, panhandling and shoplifting. A city has 30 days to respond, and after that the owner can sue (KOCO). Oklahoma City reports no claims so far. The council's three big items, the data center moratorium, the Peak on the Parkway TIF and homelessness funding, come back Dec. 2.

**National and foreign.**

**Hormuz: ten tankers hit in a week, and Iran is now blacklisting tugboats.** *Confirmed (UKMTO warnings, via gCaptain and Reuters):* three eastbound tankers in the strait were struck by unidentified projectiles. All three kept sailing and all crews are accounted for. A bulk carrier anchored off Khor Fakkan, UAE, was also hit and caught fire. That makes ten tanker strikes since Nov. 16. The International Maritime Organization counts 141 confirmed incidents and 25 seafarer deaths in the war zone. *Still unconfirmed:* Iran's claim that the ships hit mines. UKMTO says "projectiles," and Orbital Risk Intelligence says it has seen no evidence of mines. Iran's strait authority added 26 tugboats to its "non-compliant" list for servicing ships it doesn't approve. The point is to make salvage and towing a sanctions risk too. U.S.-escorted transits rose to about 38 a day, compared with roughly 138 a day in 2025, and about 218 ships are waiting at anchor (JMIC). The U.S. escorts tankers and blockades Iran's ports. Iran uses projectiles and paperwork. Neither has stopped the other.

**Security watch.**

**Megaspeed is now on the Entity List, and the AG says he doesn't know what export probe you mean.** *Confirmed (BIS final rule, FR Doc. 2026-23999, filed Nov. 23, publishing Nov. 25):* Commerce added seven firms in Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand to the Entity List. They include Megaspeed International, its Thai and Malaysian affiliates, and Apex Enterprise Solutions, an Alibaba subsidiary in Singapore. No U.S.-controlled item can go to them without a license, and license requests will be presumed denied. The stated reason is the "significant risk" that they divert advanced chips. Alibaba says Apex never diverted chips (Reuters, via CNBC). Treasury had pushed publicly for this listing over the weekend. *On the record (ABC News, DOJ press conference):* Attorney General Todd Blanche refused to say whether Huang had ever been investigated. Asked how the administration could clear Huang while the export probe is still running, he said, "When you say the export investigation is ongoing, I don't know what you're talking about." Later he called that "not new information" and called Nvidia "a good corporate citizen." Twelve days ago he described the investigation as ongoing. *Reported (NYT, via ABC):* officials assured Nvidia's board before the reinstatement that Huang faced no criminal charges. *Reported (CNBC, citing Chairman Tim Scott):* Huang will testify publicly as the only witness at a Senate Banking export-controls hearing on Dec. 3. That comes after the closed-door House interviews on Dec. 1 and 2. **Why it matters:** the government is getting tough on the Singapore front company while its top prosecutor publicly refuses to discuss the American company whose chips went through it. The President said he "made the deal," so the incentive is to keep the case narrow. Dec. 1 through 3 is when the record starts.

FAA: eleven days later, the root cause still hasn't been released. *Reported (NYT, anonymous sources):* the FAA asked L3Harris for a list of who was physically at its Melbourne network center that day. L3Harris says it was fully staffed and that the FAA approved the timing of the work.

**Economy and markets.**

**Consumers feel better, but the Fed is still expected to hike.** The Conference Board's confidence index jumped to 103.3 from 91.9, its highest since January 2025. Nearly all of the gain came from Democrats and independents after the midterms, while Republican confidence slipped (Reuters). Consumers still expect 4.5% inflation over the next year, and that is what the Fed watches. Futures put the odds of a quarter-point hike on Dec. 9 at roughly 97–100%, and the odds of another in January around 60% (CME FedWatch, via CNBC and Trading Economics). Today's 5-year Treasury auction cleared at 4.719%, slightly above the pre-auction 4.716%. That's a small sign buyers want more yield to hold government debt. The 10-year yield ended near 4.90% and the 2-year near 4.67%. October PCE inflation, GDP and the Beige Book all come out Wednesday, with headline PCE expected around 3.8%. Inflation running near 4% while the government keeps borrowing is the real story behind the sentiment number.

**Oil bounced on the attacks.** Brent settled up $2.03 at $91.40 and WTI up $1.88 at $82.05, after both hit their lowest levels since August during the day (CNBC/Reuters). The other side of the trade: Deutsche Bank estimates October inventories imply an oversupply of 1.7 million barrels a day, and strategic-reserve releases are still flowing. China and India are on pace for record Russian crude imports under temporary U.S. sanctions waivers. In other words, Washington is loosening Russian oil sanctions to make up for the barrels the Iran war takes off the market.
