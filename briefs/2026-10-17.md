**Tulsa and Oklahoma.**

**The Port of Inola fight is heading to court, and the port is the one suing.** On Thursday the Tulsa Ports board voted unanimously to hire GableGotwals and authorized a lawsuit against the Town of Inola and "any necessary Town officials." The suit would ask a judge to declare the town's ordinances and its moratorium on industrial permits invalid at the Port of Inola and to block them. That's the site of the proposed Emirates Global Aluminium (EGA) and Century aluminum smelter. As of Thursday afternoon no complaint had shown up in court records. Inola extended its smelter moratorium in September through April 7, 2027, which is the same day as a planned town referendum. Four residents have a separate federal suit trying to stop federal money for the project until environmental reviews are finished. The port also has its own pending case in Rogers County (Miller v. Tulsa Ports, CV-2026-185).

Here's the incentive. The port says it's a "non-appropriated" state agency, so no public money pays its lawyers, though it admits state and federal grants have paid for infrastructure. Either way, a state-chartered authority is suing a town of a few thousand people to override a local vote that hasn't happened yet. That's a local-control question no matter what you think of the smelter. Tulsa World headlines it as a $6.9 billion plant while TV outlets say $4 billion, and the two figures aren't reconciled here. What's still missing is the number that matters most, which is who pays for the power hookup on the PSO rate base. (Tulsa Ports agenda, primary; FOX23/KRMG and KJRH on the vote; Tulsa World headline only, paywalled. Fact / reporting.)

**Diesel: Stitt and Sen. Armstrong break with Trump on the Russia deal.** On Friday the President signed an executive order waiving the sanctions in the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, which he signed in September after it passed the Senate 86–11. Treasury's General License 135 had already let Russian diesel back onto world markets under the Oct. 9 deal with Putin. Sen. Alan Armstrong (R-OK) was one of six Republicans and six Democrats who signed an Oct. 13 letter asking him to rescind the license. Gov. Stitt told Newsweek the deal "may be prolonging a war with Ukraine for a short-term gain" and compared it to Biden draining the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. The volume is small. Russia's initial 300,000 metric tons is about 2.24 million barrels, or roughly 74,500 barrels a day if spread over a month, and diesel futures barely moved. Oklahoma diesel was $5.91 on Friday, just under September's record.

So the action here is a sanctions law signed and then waived within weeks, three weeks before a midterm, for a barrel count too small to move the price. Stitt's own fix, the dyed-diesel order for farm use, is also a carve-out from the tax system rather than more supply. (Senate letter and NBC on the executive order; Newsweek interview; AAA. Fact / reporting.)

**The state's ICE detention deal is paying for itself on paper, but the records stop in March.** The Frontier's records investigation shows how the Department of Corrections serves as the middleman between ICE and CoreCivic at the reopened Diamondback prison in Watonga, which skipped the competitive bidding a federal contract would normally require. From October through March, ICE paid $24 million. The state kept a flat $833,333 a month, about $5 million, and CoreCivic got about $19 million. ICE paid $2 million a month before any detainees arrived. Diamondback held 1,923 people on Aug. 31, out of 2,160 beds, and ICE's own figures say about 86% have no criminal history. Watonga has collected about $504,000 in per-detainee and utility fees. The open question is that DOC says it has no records of payments after March, and an official said in September the agency hadn't received any ICE money since April. If ICE isn't paying, someone is floating the bill. (The Frontier, citing invoices and bank records it obtained. Reporting; document-based.)

**Drummond drops his charter school suit.** Attorney General Gentner Drummond dismissed his case against the Statewide Charter School Board on Thursday, without prejudice, after Ben Gamla Jewish Charter School's revised application failed 4–3. It needed five votes. His filing says the plan shrank from a 335-to-400-student K–12 school to an online high school aiming for 10 students in its first year, while the budget was still built on several hundred. The religious-charter question is still unsettled, and any new application has to start over for a later school year. (AG release, primary. Fact.)

**TPS votes Monday on closing 17 schools.** The school board meets Oct. 19 to vote on closing or consolidating 14 elementaries, two middle schools, and Tulsa MET starting in 2027–28. The district projects about $11.1 million in savings over time, against a $12 million-plus operating gap, and the board can approve, change, table, or reject the plan. It's a citywide budget call, so it's worth watching. (District materials via KRMG and Tulsa Local News. Fact.)

**Election mail scares.** The State Election Board was evacuated Tuesday over an envelope of white powder that turned out to be harmless, and Sen. Lankford says police intercepted a suspicious letter to one of his offices. The FBI and the Postal Inspection Service are looking at similar mailings around the country. The board says voting isn't affected. (Election Board statement; AP. Fact.)

**National and foreign.**

**Hormuz: Iran says two supertankers hit its mines, and the U.S. says the strait is open.** On Saturday the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps navy said two tankers, each carrying about 2 million barrels, struck mines and caught fire while using an "unauthorized" route with their transponders off. It said one was left dead in the water. Nobody has confirmed it independently, and the IRGC didn't name either ship. Separately, UK Maritime Trade Operations (UKMTO) logged a tanker hit by an "unknown projectile" off Saudi Arabia on Friday, with the crew evacuated, and it counts 10 attacks in the week ending Friday and 23 so far in October. It also says it's "struggling to accurately report" because most ships no longer report attacks. CENTCOM's Adm. Brad Cooper said Friday that the strait "is controlled by the United States and our regional partners, not Iran." Both of those are words. What you can check is flows, and Kpler's last read was 12.6 million barrels a day, against about 17 million before the war. Markets reopen Sunday night. If the mine claims hold up, insurers will respond before any statement does. (IRNA via Reuters, unconfirmed; UKMTO via CBS/AP; CENTCOM. Claims are claims.)

**Economics and geopolitics.**

**The IMF's verdict is resilient, but with thinner buffers.** The International Monetary and Financial Committee wrapped up in Bangkok saying the global economy "has weathered the latest in a series of shocks better than expected, but buffers have been eroded." It named rising sovereign bond yields in "systemic economies" and high refinancing needs as risks, and it called for fiscal consolidation and central-bank independence. It's a consensus document with no money or enforcement attached, but it's notable that a 25-member committee of finance ministers and central bankers put bond-market stress in writing in the same week the U.S. 10-year sat near 5.1%. Delegates also walked out on a Russian official. (IMFC chair's statement, primary; CNBC. Fact / opinion.)
