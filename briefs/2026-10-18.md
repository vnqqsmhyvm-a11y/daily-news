**Tulsa and Oklahoma.**

**Tulsa police will take questions on license-plate cameras Monday, but only questions sent in ahead of time.** The department holds a public session on its automated license plate readers Monday, Oct. 19, from 6 to 7 p.m. at Tulsa Tech near 31st and Memorial. The panel will answer questions submitted in advance, not from the floor. TPD says it can tap about 270 plate-reading cameras in the city but owns only 108. The rest belong to businesses, neighborhood associations and residents who share their data with police. Police credit the cameras with a drop in auto theft and faster homicide cases, and say they don't stream live or capture faces.

The questions TPD itself expects are the right ones: where the data goes, who can search it, and whether officers use it properly. State law says plate-reader data can't be used for anything other than enforcing the compulsory insurance law "or as otherwise permitted by law," and that last clause does all the work. The count also keeps moving. TPD's own website has listed about 75 readers and live cameras, and The Frontier reported access to nearly 300 last year. In August, Flock cut its default retention for police to seven days from 30, and Coweta's police chief moved to pull his town's two cameras after residents objected. A count of searches run and of data shared outside TPD would say more than Monday's slides. (News On 6; Tulsa Flyer on state law and Coweta. Reporting.)

**Inola: the port voted to sue, but there's no word of a filing yet.** As of Saturday's coverage, no one reported a complaint filed after Thursday's Tulsa Ports board vote, and both the port and Inola's mayor declined comment. The port says it has built more than $100 million in infrastructure at the Port of Inola over seven years. That's the sunk cost driving the fight, and one resident called it "a battle of pocketbooks" a small town can't match. (News 9 / News On 6. Reporting.)

**SQ 847, in numbers.** On Nov. 3, voters decide whether to lower the yearly cap on assessed-value growth from 3% to 1.75% for homesteads and farmland, and from 5% to 4% for other property, starting with tax year 2027. It doesn't cut rates or current bills. It slows how fast the taxable base can climb, so a $200,000 homestead could be assessed at no more than $203,500 the next year. It also adds tighter tiered caps for homeowners 65 and older whose income is above the senior-freeze limit. Speaker Kyle Hilbert and Pro Tem Lonnie Paxton sponsored it, the Farm Bureau backs it, and legislative Democrats voted no. The official ballot title says the fiscal impact is unknown. OK Policy, which urges a no vote, estimates $42 million less local revenue in year one and $241 million by year five. Oklahoma City says it could lose $683 million in bonding capacity over 20 years.

The catch for taxpayers is that the cap limits the base, not the rate. Counties and school districts can still raise millage, and bond debt already on the books still has to be paid. So the relief is real only if local governments live within slower growth. No money had been reported for or against the measure as of Oct. 8, and pre-general reports are due Oct. 26. (Oklahoma Watch via KGOU; OK Policy is an advocacy shop. Fact / opinion.)

**Treasurer: a career auditor against a ballot-access placeholder.** State Auditor Cindy Byrd (R), who beat incumbent Treasurer Todd Russ with nearly 62% in the June primary, faces Libertarian Kiefer Perry of Broken Arrow, a first-time candidate who says he doesn't expect to win. He's running so Libertarians keep ballot access, which takes 2.5% statewide. Byrd has spent nearly 30 years in the auditor's office, and Trump endorsed her Oct. 1. This is a stewardship office, so her audit record matters more than the endorsement. (Oklahoma Voice. Reporting.)

**Childcare: a three-way split is pitched, with no bill or price tag yet.** Rep. Suzanne Schreiber (D-Tulsa) says she'll file a bill next session in which the state, the employer and the parent each pay a third, for example $300 apiece on a $900 month. The state would pay only if the employer does. Supporters point to Michigan, which has 310 employers, more than 1,100 children and about $14 million "saved" over four years, but those are an advocate's figures. And "saved" means shifted to employers and taxpayers, not removed. Income limits and the funding source aren't set. It's an interim study, so it's words, not a vote. (KHBS. Reporting.)

**National and foreign.**

**The Russia sanctions order waives every tariff it creates.** Friday's executive order is now on the White House site, and the text is plain. Section 2 raises duties on Russian goods by 500% and sets a 100% duty on the top five buyers of Russian crude or gas that knowingly make new purchases on or after today, Oct. 18. Section 4(a) then waives "any duties provided for in sections 112 and 113" of the Graham Act, which is all of them. Sections 4(b) and 4(c) waive most of the law's sanctions provisions too. The waivers last until the President or the Secretary of State decides otherwise. Russia sanctions under earlier executive orders stay in force.

TFTC's newsdesk wrote Friday that "the tariff machinery is real" and that the waiver's scope needed a full read. The full read says the tariffs were switched off the day they were switched on. Congress voted 86–11 in the Senate for mandatory sanctions and wrote in the waiver authority itself, and that waiver is how the White House keeps the threat as leverage on a Russia deal without paying for it at the pump. Today's purchase date only matters if a waiver is lifted. (White House order text, primary; TFTC Newsdesk. Fact / analysis.)

**Hormuz: Iran restates its terms, and the oil market shrugs.** Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, said Sunday that Tehran's mid-September offer to reopen the strait within seven days is "still on the table." The conditions include lifting the U.S. blockade of Iranian ports and the sanctions on its oil exports, and ending hostilities on every front. Washington's sticking point, per Vice President Vance earlier this month, is a "meaningful" cut in Iran's uranium enrichment. CENTCOM says it has turned back 138 commercial vessels since resuming the blockade on July 14. The Revolutionary Guard's claim that two tankers hit mines is still unconfirmed, and the ships are still unnamed. Brent reopened Sunday evening at $110.25 and was about $109 by 5:20 p.m. CT, against Friday's $109.86 settle. The market didn't see anything new in the weekend's claims. (Reuters via AAP; CENTCOM via Kurdistan24; Yahoo Finance. Claims are claims.)
