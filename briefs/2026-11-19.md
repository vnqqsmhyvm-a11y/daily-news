**Tulsa and Oklahoma.**

**Council approves the police drone pilot. Peak on the Parkway TIF and the data-center pause both slide to Dec. 2.** At Wednesday's meeting the council voted 8–0 to accept a $775,145 Tulsa Police Foundation donation for a three-year police drone pilot. It also voted 7–0 to spend $6.09 million on economic and neighborhood items, including the BOK East Side entrepreneurship center and after-school programs (City of Tulsa council meeting, via Oklahoma Record's meeting record). The Peak on the Parkway plan got its first public hearing: a roughly $200 million mixed-use project with about 300 homes, medical offices and retail. It would carry four new tax-increment districts expected to capture about $100 million over 25 years, with about $28 million of that going to public infrastructure. Adoption is set for Dec. 2. Councilors agreed in principle to extend the hyperscale data-center moratorium to March 31, or until zoning rules pass if that comes first, but the ordinance itself was continued to the next agenda. Two things to watch. The drone program is a surveillance tool paid for with private money, and the vote approved the donation, not a public use policy. On the TIF, roughly $72 million of the $100 million captured doesn't go to public infrastructure, so ask where it goes. Separately, the developers of "Project Nexus" have filed a preliminary application for another TIF district covering more than 22 blocks of northwest downtown (Tulsa World, paywalled, headline only). That would make two big TIF asks in one month.

**Grand jury petition on the DA is in.** Grand Jury Justice filed 8,083 signatures before Thursday's deadline. The petition seeks a county grand jury on 11 areas of the Tulsa County DA's office, ranging from open-records compliance to conflicts of interest (News On 6, citing court records). The election board secretary now has to verify the signatures. Nothing happens unless the certified count clears the legal bar.

**SPP kills a $72 million central Oklahoma line.** The Southwest Power Pool board withdrew the Mathewson–Redbud project, a roughly 40-mile 345-kV line between Piedmont and Luther, after staff said other planned projects could meet the need for less. The estimated cost had climbed to $89.5 million (KGOU/KOSU). That's a rare case of a grid planner cutting cost instead of piling it onto the rate base. Watch whether the replacement projects come in cheaper or just come in later.

**Inola smelter air study.** A 336-page modeling report filed with DEQ says the proposed aluminum smelter at the Port of Inola would have little to no effect on air quality. Opponents dispute it (Tulsa World, paywalled, headline only). DEQ had already sent the developer back for more support on its emissions and ozone math, and the AG has asked for ownership and financing records. That report was prepared for the developer, so DEQ's ruling on it is what counts.

Also: four western Oklahoma towns (Alva, Elk City, Mangum, Altus) told the state Commerce Department they want to host a federally backed nuclear plant (The Oklahoman, via OK Policy). That's a letter of interest, nothing built yet.

**National and foreign.**

**Shenzhen produces a declaration, not a deal.** The White House posted the U.S.–Russia–China "Declaration toward Peace." It has four points: reform the UN, increase refined-fuel supply, insist the ICC "must not abuse" its jurisdiction over state officials, and hold a "dialogue" on nuclear risk. It names no arms-control talks and doesn't mention Ukraine or Iran (White House text; Reuters). Judged by actions, the concrete items are a fuel pledge with no volumes and a shared position against international courts. All three governments have reasons to want that last one.

**Hormuz: Iran says two supertankers hit mines.** The IRGC Navy says two unnamed tankers carrying more than 4 million barrels struck mines on an "unauthorized route" and caught fire. Nobody has independently verified it, and the ships haven't been identified (Anadolu, Al-Monitor). If true, Iran is claiming it controls which lanes are legal. Crude barely moved: WTI settled at $83.21 and Brent at $91.98 (Newsquawk). Traders are still weighing a global surplus against the claim.

**Security watch.**

**Washington's verdict on the outage cluster: separate failures, not an attack.** *Confirmed (sworn House Homeland Security testimony, 11/18):* DNI Jay Clayton said that "based on what we know today… these are separate service outages associated with maintenance and technical changes. They are not a coordinated or state-sponsored attack." DHS Secretary Mullin told the same committee he was "98% confident" adversaries weren't behind them (AP). *Speculation:* Sen. Schumer says it's "too coincidental" and wants an intelligence red team to report by Dec. 1. He hasn't offered any evidence, and he's campaigning to become majority leader. **Why it matters:** the administration now owns that conclusion on the record. If any of these turns out to be an attack, the testimony becomes the story.

**NSA network: still not right on day three.** *Confirmed (NSA, 11/17):* a "temporary degradation during scheduled routine maintenance." *Reported (NBC, two sources, 11/19):* NSA Net was still down Thursday, nonessential staff were told to work from home for a third straight day, and a Georgia site handling Middle East intercept work was affected too. Collection is said to be unaffected. That contradicts Wednesday's CBS report that the network was back up. The Record's source said it's running but logins are staggered because they're the heaviest load on the system. **Why it matters:** the agency that tells the rest of government how to patch couldn't patch itself without a multi-day outage.

**FAA ground stop: a cause is promised this week.** *Reported (Reuters):* FAA Administrator Bedford said Thursday the government expects to announce the root cause this week, with Secretary Duffy doing the briefing, and he called the FTI network L3Harris has run since 2002 "patchwork, archaic." *Confirmed (FAA Q&A):* the flood was 1,000 connection attempts every 4–5 seconds, and a broadcast function has been disabled on all the new routers. When the cause comes out, check whether it names a configuration choice and who made it.

**Nvidia: California opens its own probe.** *Confirmed (California AG release, 11/19):* Attorney General Bonta served Nvidia with an investigative subpoena. He's asking whether advanced chips or confidential information reached China and whether Nvidia misled investors. Federally there are still no charges and no Megaspeed entity listing. *Reported, from an interested party:* short seller Hunterbrook alleges that blacklisted Chinese server maker IEIT kept shipping newer AMD firmware after its 2025 listing. Hunterbrook holds a short position, AMD denies the claim, and no agency has acted. Treat it as a lead, not a finding, even though AMD's stock fell about 8%.

Microsoft 365: the final post-incident report is due Friday.

**Economy and markets.**

**Anthropic pops, and everything else sells.** ANT opened at $111.50, 34% above its $83 IPO price, and closed at $97.17, up 17%. The IPO raised $81.3 billion, and the fully diluted value is about $1.8 trillion (CNBC). Indexes hit their session lows the minute it started trading. The Dow fell 1,101 points (2.2%) to 49,097, its first close below 50,000 since June. The S&P lost 2.0% to 7,175.83, its sixth straight loss, and the Nasdaq lost 2.25%. VIX touched about 29. Plain version: insiders and early backers got their exit at a record price while the broader AI trade fell. That's what the end of a cycle looks like, though one day doesn't prove it.

**Fed talks overheating.** Chicago Fed President Goolsbee said the Fed's job is to keep the AI investment surge from overheating the economy, and he blamed softer job growth on immigration policy, not weakness (CoinDesk's account of his remarks). Jobless claims rose to 203,000, and the Philly Fed index fell to 6.3 from 20.9, with new orders slightly negative. Prices paid jumped to 58.2, the highest since April. That mix of slower activity and rising input costs is the stagflation pattern. The 10-year was about 5.05%. Gold fell about 2% to roughly $3,667, its weakest since September 2025.

**Bitcoin.** It dropped under $62,000, the lowest since early July, and is headed for a seventh straight weekly loss. It's now more than 7% below its 200-week moving average (CoinDesk). Strategy's 12% preferred, STRC, has slipped below par, and Saylor says the company will keep buying it back under $100. That's the treasury-company model being tested in public. No policy or protocol move.
