**Tulsa and Oklahoma.** The long-promised Tulsa–Jenks low-water dam is short on money. According to the Tulsa Flyer's account of a Nov. 9 town hall, Tulsa, Jenks, the Muscogee Nation and INCOG have pledged about $105 million, including about $62.2 million from Tulsa's Vision 2016 sales tax. The four versions on the table cost $143.5 million (dam only) to $194 million (the plan voters approved, with parks and a pedestrian bridge). That leaves a gap of roughly $38 million to $89 million, depending on how much gets cut. Officials are looking at Tulsa's leftover Vision economic-development money, private donors and the state. If the dam dies, officials say the money is already spoken for: Jenks shifts its share to other city infrastructure, and Tulsa must put about $18 million into a Zink Dam maintenance endowment and split the rest among river projects. A steering committee meets in early December. The question for taxpayers: voters approved a $194 million project in 2016, and the cheaper versions drop the amenities they were promised. Council takes up the Peak on the Parkway tax-increment hearing Wednesday at 5 p.m.

**Faith, order, money, power.** The Nvidia scandal produced its first government action. Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security issued an interim final rule Monday. It requires a license for overseas data centers using advanced US chips to sell significant computing power to companies headquartered in China (or Macau and other countries of concern). Data centers already doing that have 90 days from the effective date to get one. The rule also requires a license to ship advanced chips to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, which the rule calls higher-than-normal diversion risks. Per BIS's release as reported by CNBC, the rule was "written by and at the direction of" Treasury and the White House National Security Council. That's unusual, and it tells you Commerce was overruled. Treasury Secretary Bessent said Friday that Treasury, the Pentagon, State and the US Trade Representative had wanted Megaspeed blacklisted for months and Commerce hadn't acted. He's now calling for an outside CEO at Nvidia. Incentive: every agency wants to be the one that was right before the scandal. Lutnick's Commerce Department is the one that has to explain why it waited.

**Security watch.** *Microsoft 365, new facts: cause found.* Microsoft's preliminary post-incident report, reproduced by Office Watch and summarized by Ars Technica, says three things combined. First, a gateway code defect shipped in July went undetected because the test hardware had fewer CPU cores than production machines. Second, Teams traffic was shifted onto the primary gateway about 15 hours before the outage. Third, the gateway fleet was already running 14% below normal capacity. More than 80% of affected gateway servers froze. Restarts and rollbacks didn't work because the bug was already in the earlier version. The outage ran from 10:20 a.m. CT Nov. 12 to 10:10 a.m. CT Nov. 13. Microsoft lists the final status as "not related to cyber or AI/agents." Still open: why the regional isolation failed. The final report is due Nov. 20. Why it matters: one dormant bug took down email for much of the working world for a day, with no attacker involved.

*FAA ground stop.* No root cause yet. Secretary Duffy told ABC that 69 of the new routers are in the airspace system and all have broadcasting turned off. The FAA's written Q&A says about 60. He said the open question is "user error or installer error from L3Harris" versus a faulty router. HPE, which owns router maker Juniper, says the device "functioned as it was configured to by the installer." That puts the blame back on the contractor's setup. Still open: the lab finding, and whether Congress holds a hearing.

*Chase slowdown.* JPMorgan Chase said a temporary software bug in its own on-premise systems slowed consumer transactions Monday. It said the problem wasn't cyber-related, customers kept access to their funds, and it aimed to resolve it by day's end (company statement via AP and CNBC). Customers at other banks saw delays only on payments touching Chase, including some Zelle transfers. It's the third big infrastructure failure in five days, and none so far traces to an attacker.
