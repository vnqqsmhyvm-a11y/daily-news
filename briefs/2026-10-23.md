**Tulsa and Oklahoma.**

**The attorney general shut down a third vote on the Jewish charter school, and the board appealed a ruling it had backed.** Attorney General Gentner Drummond issued a binding opinion Thursday saying state law gives the Statewide Charter School Board a "closed, two-step" process for new online charters, with "no third vote, rehearing, reconsideration, or rescission." The board had called a Friday special meeting to try again on Ben Gamla Jewish Charter School, which got four yes votes on Oct. 12, one short of the five it needed. After the opinion, it didn't vote on the application. It did vote to appeal to the Tenth Circuit the September federal ruling in Ben Gamla's lawsuit, a ruling the board had supported. The AG's office called that "strange, to say the least." The opinion was requested by one Democrat and one Republican in the House. Ben Gamla's founder, former U.S. Rep. Peter Deutsch, had written that without both approvals the school would withdraw, and threatened contempt and personal liability for board members. The incentive: an appeals ruling settles the religious-charter question for more than one school, which matters more to both sides than this application. Whether Ben Gamla now withdraws is the next thing to watch. (Oklahoma Voice; Oct. 23 board agenda, primary; KOCO. AG opinion text not opened. Fact.)

**Tulsa's first IKEA opened Friday, built partly on $2.5 million in city sales-tax money.** Hundreds lined up at Tulsa Hills, some from 3 a.m. The city council approved the incentive in March under the Vision Tulsa retail program. The money went to the shopping-center developer, not IKEA, for building, parking and public infrastructure work, with an emergency clause. The city's economic development director projected $30 million to $40 million a year in sales, against about $3 million from the Belk it replaced, and $100 million to $120 million in "indirect impacts." Those are the city's projections, not results. The real test is the sales-tax receipts from that site a year from now, and whether they come from new shoppers or from money moved from other Tulsa stores. (KTUL; KJRH; Tulsa Flyer. Fact / city projection.)

**Nine appellate judges are on the Nov. 3 retention ballot.** Supreme Court Justices M. John Kane IV, Travis Jett, Dana Kuehn and Richard Darby; Robert Hudson of the Court of Criminal Appeals; and Court of Civil Appeals Judges Stacie Hixon, Jane Wiseman, Deborah Barnes and James Huber. A no vote creates a vacancy that the governor fills from the nominating commission's list of three. Voters have refused to retain a judge once: Justice Yvonne Kauger in 2024, after conservative groups funded by undisclosed donors campaigned against three justices. Jett is the Stitt appointee who replaced her. State Question 845, on the same ballot, could change parts of that appointment process. The judges' majority opinions are posted by name on the Oklahoma Supreme Court Network, which is the record to read, since retention judges can't campaign. (Tulsa Flyer. Fact.)

**National and foreign.**

**The administration is saying out loud that the Iran war may run another year, and Brent hit its highest close since April.** U.N. Ambassador Mike Waltz told the Financial Times the war could last another year and that the blockade can be held "for the foreseeable future." President Trump said "We could take the whole country" and "the Hormuz Strait is ours," while keeping his line of no new strikes before the midterms. Ambassador Tom Barrack undercut that: "Could there be a strike this weekend? Absolutely." Those are words. The deed is a blockade still in place. Iranian state TV claims "non-compliant" traffic through Hormuz is fully halted, and Trump calls Iran's claims of hitting ships fake. Neither claim is confirmed. What is confirmed is an oil slick of about 60 square miles off the UAE coast, which analysts tie to a tanker hit on Oct. 9. Saudi Aramco says its East-West pipeline, which carries crude around Hormuz to the Red Sea, now moves 7 million barrels a day, up from 2 million. A missile was intercepted over Riyadh, Britain committed fighter jets to defend Saudi Arabia, and the death toll from the Dammam airport strike is now two. Foreign ministers from eight countries, including Britain, France, Germany, Turkey and Saudi Arabia, met in Istanbul and urged restraint. Iran's president goes to Moscow next week. Midterm timing explains the dueling claims: Tehran gains from high pump prices before Nov. 3, and Washington gains from saying oil is flowing. (CBS News live coverage citing FT, AP and Reuters; Arab News; Xinhua. Fact / claims labeled.)

**Economics and geopolitics.**

**Oil kept climbing while the Fed's inflation problem got harder.** December Brent settled at $116.72, up $3.55 and 6% on the week. WTI settled at $103.27. Oklahoma diesel averaged about $5.92 a gallon and regular about $3.95. The 10-year Treasury yield bounced back to about 5.00% after Thursday's big drop, and the 2-year rose to about 4.69%. Michigan consumer sentiment was 48, down 10% from a year ago, with expected inflation at 4.7% for the next year and 3.5% over five years, both up slightly. Factory PMI, a survey of purchasing managers, was 56, its highest in 53 months. The Dow fell 1.2% and the S&P 500 fell 0.5%, while energy stocks hit a record. Gold slipped under $4,000. The Fed meets Oct. 28. (Reuters; WSJ; CNBC; AAA via search summary. Fact.)

**Trump's housing order trims fees and leaves rates alone.** The Oct. 22 order tells the housing finance regulator to pilot automatic cancellation of mortgage insurance on new Fannie and Freddie loans once a computer valuation shows enough equity. It also makes permanent a pilot waiving title insurance requirements, tells USDA and the VA to cut rates on their own direct home loans, and sets up a 180-day task force on factory-built housing. It doesn't touch the 30-year rate, which eased to 7.32% this week from 7.40%. Pending home sales fell 7.3% to a record low, September single-family starts fell 4.6%, and builder sentiment is at 29. Signed 12 days before the midterms, the order spends little and promises recommendations. Who pays for a cheaper government direct loan is the taxpayer, and the mortgage-insurance pilot depends on automated valuations holding up if prices fall. (White House order text, primary; FRED; TD Economics. Fact / analysis labeled.)
