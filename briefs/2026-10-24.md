**Tulsa and Oklahoma.** Brent Swadley will not go to prison. Oklahoma County District Judge Cindy Truong gave him a six-year suspended sentence, $1.2 million in restitution and a $50,000 fine, passing over the 10 years the jury recommended when it convicted him in May on six felonies: conspiracy to defraud the state and five counts of false claims. The case is the state-park restaurant contract with the Tourism Department, which paid his chain about $16 million before it was canceled in 2022 (facts from the Attorney General's own release). Restitution is due by Dec. 1, 2028, per KOCO. Attorney General Gentner Drummond says the judge "should resign." Both candidates to replace him, Jon Echols and Nick Coffey, criticized the sentence but declined to join the resignation call. What was done: a jury convicted, a judge declined to imprison. What was said: a resignation demand from an official leaving office in January, which carries no legal force. The money question nobody has answered in public is how much of the $16 million was actually inflated billing, and therefore whether $1.2 million makes taxpayers whole. In Tulsa, a quiet Saturday with no council or county action.

**Iran and the Gulf.** The deeds are pointing toward another round of strikes, while the words go both ways. On Saturday Trump told reporters the U.S. could strike "tomorrow" or reach "a nice big beautiful agreement," and said the choice is "up to them." On Friday his ambassador to Turkey, Tom Barrack, said a strike could "absolutely" come this weekend. The Wall Street Journal reports sailors on the USS George Washington strike group loading munitions. CNN reports diplomats and families being moved out of Lebanon, the UAE, Qatar and Jordan. The New York Times reports a short, intense U.S.-Israeli campaign planned for after the Nov. 3 midterms. Those three reports reached me secondhand through Iran International's live blog, so treat them as credible reporting, not confirmed. On the water, Iran's Revolutionary Guard claims two supertankers carrying more than 4.5 million barrels hit mines on the "unauthorized" southern route and were abandoned. It named no ships, and U.S. Central Command says there are no mines in the strait, so this is a claim, not a fact. The confirmed pattern is bad enough. The Washington Institute counts 28 attacks on shipping around Hormuz in October, the most of any month on record, and 113 since the war began Feb. 28. Reuters' review of the IMO list finds more than 30 supertankers damaged, 11 of them this month. Treasury Secretary Bessent says Gulf oil exports are now above pre-war levels. That claim is hard to square with a record month of attacks, and he showed no data. The incentive on both sides is the midterm calendar. Iran wants pump prices high before Nov. 3, and Washington's public line is that the war ends after the vote.

**Money and markets.** Oil closed the week at a six-month high. December Brent settled at $116.72, up about 6% on the week, and WTI at $103.27. Exxon, Chevron and ConocoPhillips all closed at record highs. On Treasury's par curve the 10-year ended Friday at 5.00%, up 4 basis points on the day but down 9 for the week. The 2-year was 4.69% and the 30-year 5.33%. The bond rally was a run to safety on weak sentiment and a tech selloff, not a sign that inflation is cooling. S&P Global's October survey found the fastest rise in selling prices since August 2022, and Michigan's one-year inflation expectation rose to 4.7%. The 30-year mortgage average is 7.32%. The Fed decides Wednesday from a 3.75%–4.00% range, and TD Economics argues firm growth and sticky inflation make the case for another hike this year (their opinion). The odd signal is gold: futures settled near $4,008, down from about $4,166 a week earlier and far below the 52-week high of about $5,417. With 5% nominal yields, holding metal is costly, so gold is not confirming the inflation scare that oil and the price surveys are flashing. That is my interpretation, not a settled fact. Bitcoin is near $80,700 with no policy or protocol news.
