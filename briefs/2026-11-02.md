**Tulsa and Oklahoma.** Election Day is tomorrow. Polls are open 7 a.m. to 7 p.m., you vote at your assigned precinct, and you need a photo ID or a state voter ID card. The ballot is long, so the county election board suggests pulling your sample ballot from the OK Voter Portal first (KTUL). Tulsa County had more than 26,000 early in-person voters over four days, up from about 16,300 in 2022 and slightly above 2024's 25,954. The county opened four early-voting sites for the first time, and the board itself credits the extra sites for much of the jump (KTUL; News On 6). Statewide, about 186,500 people voted early in person and another 42,669 mailed absentee ballots had come back, for more than 220,000 votes already cast (KOCO, from State Election Board figures). Party registration of early voters tells you who showed up, not how they voted.

The other vote that hits your wallet happens tomorrow afternoon, and it isn't on the ballot. The Corporation Commission meets at 1:30 p.m. Tuesday to vote on PSO's rate case (OCC special-meeting agenda). The draft order raises every customer class's total bill by at least 1.5%, with residential likely in the high-1% to mid-2% range, and makes PSO refund the part of its interim rates that ran above what the order allows, within 90 days (FOX23). Commissioner Hiett says residential will land well below the roughly 5% in an earlier proposal. That is the pattern to remember: an opening ask that settles far lower was padding. What matters for the long run is what the order does with large-load customers like data centers, and that won't be clear until the order is out. A Commission seat is also on your ballot.

On energy, the two governor candidates gave KOCO their answers. Munson says data centers and other heavy users should carry their own power costs, and that she has opposed proposed rate increases at the Commission. Mazzei says the Commission is too slow on drilling permits, claiming nine to 18 months in Oklahoma against 16 to 90 days in Texas. That comparison is his claim; we haven't checked it against Commission data. Both are words. Neither office-seeker has a vote at the Commission.

DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin was in Tulsa touting Operation Guardian, a three-day sweep across 15 states aimed at people holding commercial driver's licenses they weren't eligible for. ICE says it made more than 200 arrests, but only 40 of those were unauthorized drivers (KJRH). That gap between the headline number and the target is worth noticing. Mullin also said repeat-offender trucking firms and brokers "will eventually" be pursued. That's a promise, not an action yet.

New state laws took effect Nov. 1, including one letting prosecutors combine two or more DUIs within 12 months into a felony even if the first case isn't resolved yet (News On 6). Separately, Gov. Stitt has nominated 104 census tracts, 22 of them in Tulsa, for the new round of federal Opportunity Zones. Those are capital-gains tax breaks for investing there. They're nominations only until Treasury certifies them, and they would run from 2027 through 2036 (Oklahoma Department of Commerce; KOSU). Who ends up benefiting depends on which tracts make it and who already owns land in them.

**Faith, order, money, power.** Iran's talks with Qatari mediators in Doha ended without a breakthrough, but contacts continue. Qatari mediators flew to Tehran Monday night with a draft deal (Reuters). Tehran now says its seven-step plan, which Trump rejected in September, could be done in two days instead of seven. The order hasn't changed: the US ends the blockade of Iranian ports, Iran reopens Hormuz, the US waives oil sanctions and drops new ones, and only then do nuclear talks happen. Iran's Foreign Ministry said no when asked whether it would ship its enriched uranium abroad (Reuters; Euronews). Iranian state media says the supreme leader gave the government "authorities" to pursue talks. After markets closed, Trump twice answered "No" when NBC asked whether he plans to strike Iran after the midterms (NBC News). The incentive on his side is plain: falling gas prices the day before an election. On Iran's side, the plan is the same one with a shorter clock. It's still words until a ship moves or the blockade lifts. On Friday, a Pakistan-bound Qatari LNG tanker was hit by missiles near the strait just hours after Tehran cleared it for passage (Guardian), which shows how little a promise of safe passage is worth right now.

**Economics in plain language.** Oil had its biggest one-day drop since July. Brent fell 7.6% to $104.77 and US crude 7.3% to $96.46, purely on diplomacy headlines, with no barrels added (NBC News). Diesel futures fell 5.4%, but the national average was still $6.29 a gallon Monday morning, up 77% this year. Diesel is what moves your groceries.

Factories are running hot. ISM's October manufacturing index rose to 55.5 from 54.5, against forecasts of no change. Production hit 59, the highest since November 2021. Prices paid eased to 75.2 but are still very high, which means input costs are still climbing, just a bit slower (ISM). Put that next to September's 3.6% PCE inflation and the Fed's two hike votes last week. A strong economy with sticky prices is the case for a December hike, not a cut. That's a forecast. Friday's jobs report, with forecasters around 65,000, is the next real test.

**Bitcoin.** Binance published its full zero-knowledge proof of reserves for the Oct. 31 snapshot a day early. It covers 10 major tokens, and bitcoin shows 100.04% backing: 692,489 BTC held against 692,187 owed to customers (Binance proof-of-reserves page; Binance recovery update via Crypto Briefing). This is a dated, scoped snapshot users can check against their own balance, not an audit. Customers took out a net $3.64 billion after withdrawals reopened, more withdrawals open Tuesday, and a broader snapshot comes Nov. 4 (CoinDesk). The FBI has formally attributed the $6.3 billion theft to North Korea's TraderTraitor group. Federal agencies are also leaning on Thorchain node operators to block the hackers' addresses, which is a test of whether a "neutral" protocol bends to enforcement. Bitcoin was around $76,700, down about 1.5% on the day, and spot ETFs lost $643 million (Binance; CoinDesk).
