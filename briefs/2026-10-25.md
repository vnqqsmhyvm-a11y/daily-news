**Tulsa and Oklahoma.** Early voting for the Nov. 3 general runs this Wednesday through Saturday: 8 a.m. to 6 p.m. Oct. 28–30 and 8 a.m. to 2 p.m. Oct. 31. Tulsa County has four sites: the Election Board on East Skelly Drive, Tulsa Tech in Broken Arrow, and two new ones at O'Brien Park and Chandler Park. Election Day polls are open 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. Bring a photo ID that expires after the election. (Tulsa County Election Board; State Election Board calendar.)

Two state questions are on the ballot. SQ 845 rewrites the Judicial Nominating Commission (covered Oct. 21). SQ 847 (SJR 39) would tighten how fast assessed property values can rise for tax purposes. Per Ballotpedia's summary, the cap drops from 3% to 1.75% a year for homesteads and farmland and from 5% to 4% for other property, starting in 2027, with income-based tiers for seniors. What the summary doesn't answer: a cap slows the valuation, not the millage. Schools and counties that live on property tax get slower revenue growth or push for higher rates. Read the actual ballot title on your Voter Portal sample ballot before you vote.

The governor's race, record against pitch (KGOU/KOSU, Lionel Ramos). Republican Mike Mazzei chaired Senate Finance and wrote SB 315 in 2009, which removed revenue triggers and cleared a cut in the top income-tax rate from 5.5% to 5.25%. Now he promises a zero income tax and no property tax for seniors; the piece shows no plan for replacing the revenue. KGOU also notes he switched to supporting the UAE-backed Inola smelter. Democrat Cyndi Munson, the House minority leader, voted against last year's income-tax cut (HB 2764), backed the 2024 grocery-tax cut, and promises a day-one moratorium on new data centers. By LegisOK's count, 64 of Mazzei's 218 measures became law, and 23 of Munson's 163. The bill numbers are the deeds; the rest is campaign copy.

The statehouse math that matters: Republicans hold 81–18 in the House and 39–8 in the Senate (Ballotpedia). Democrats would need to gain 14 House seats or 8 Senate seats to break the two-thirds supermajority, which is what lets the Legislature override vetoes and send questions to the ballot on its own.

Tulsa itself is quiet. The City Council meets Wednesday at 5 p.m., and the filed agenda is routine: appointments, property disposal, special-event permits, and zoning. There's no Flock camera item. The city holds a general public-safety forum Monday at 5:30 p.m. at Tulsa Tech's Lemley campus on South Memorial.

**Iran and the Gulf.** Trump said Sunday "there will not be a strike today," a day after saying the U.S. might "finish the job" or cut a deal (AP, Al Jazeera). Those are words; no order has been announced. Ambassador Tom Barrack's "absolutely" possible weekend strike hadn't happened as of Sunday evening. What was actually done: CENTCOM commander Adm. Brad Cooper flew over the Strait of Hormuz and said the main shipping lanes are clear of mines, claiming more than 3,000 commercial transits and 1.5 billion barrels of crude moved with U.S. support over six months (CENTCOM, via Times of Israel). That's the military grading its own work, not independent ship tracking. Iran says messages are still passing through intermediaries, and Qatari mediators were in Tehran (Al Jazeera).

The Saudi-led coalition says it shot down two drones and a ballistic missile aimed at Riyadh, ten ballistic missiles fired at the south, and a cruise missile aimed at Khamis Mushait (Saudi Press Agency, via CNBC). The Houthis claim they hit Aramco's Yanbu facilities. Saudi Arabia hasn't confirmed any damage and Aramco hasn't commented, so treat it as a claim. Riyadh's main airport reopened its terminals after the Oct. 10 strike that killed at least 13.

The incentive on both sides is the calendar. The New York Times reported U.S. and Israeli plans for new strikes after the Nov. 3 midterms (via CNBC). Energy Aspects' Amrita Sen thinks after is likelier than before; that's her opinion, not reporting.

**Money and markets.** Oil futures reopen at 5 p.m. Central tonight. Friday's settles: Brent $116.72, its highest since April 29 and up 6.2% on the week, and WTI $103.27 (CNBC, citing settlement data). If your ticker shows Brent near $110 to $111, that's mostly the switch to the January contract on Sunday (Investing.com), not a crash. Later-dated oil being that much cheaper means the market is paying up for barrels now. The swing fact for Monday is Yanbu: the East-West pipeline to Yanbu, about 7 million barrels a day, has carried Saudi exports since Hormuz closed, and a Houthi hit in September shut loadings for about a week.

It's Fed week. The decision comes Wednesday at 1 p.m. Central with the funds rate at 3.75–4.00%, and prediction markets price a hold around 95% (Polymarket). The 10-year Treasury closed Friday at 5.00% and the 2-year at 4.69%. Thursday at 7:30 a.m. Central brings third-quarter GDP and September PCE inflation, with consensus at 3.6% a year and 3.1% core. Watch what the Fed does, not what it says: if fuel keeps pushing PCE up, the December hike Polymarket now prices at about 76% stops being a bet and becomes the plan.
