Tulsa and Oklahoma

The Oklahoma Supreme Court voted 5–4 on Tuesday to throw out SB 1027, the Legislature’s 2025 overhaul of the initiative petition process. The main target was the county caps: no county could supply more than 11.5% of its last governor’s-race vote toward a statutory petition, or 20.8% toward a constitutional one. The majority held that those caps “pose broad First Amendment problems.” Because the law’s parts couldn’t be separated, the whole thing is void (NonDoc; Oklahoma Voice). In practice, signature drives go back to the old rules. Groups that live off ballot questions just got their main tool back, and the Legislature’s work to restrict them is gone. A new Legislature, sworn in Monday and Tuesday, can try again.

On the Inola smelter, Attorney General Drummond asked a Rogers County judge on Monday to make the two companies behind it answer discovery questions. He wants to know who owns them, where the Emirati money comes from, and whether Jared Kushner or any public official or official’s family member has a stake (AG filing release). His filing calls the Kushner payment reports “allegations, not findings.” The numbers in the filing: UAE state-owned EGA owns 60% and Century Aluminum 40%. The project would get up to $500 million in federal money and about $255 million in state rebates. Governor-elect Mazzei backs the project and promises an independent air-quality evaluator. AG-elect Jon Echols has said he’ll keep the lawsuit going. The Cherokee Nation passed a resolution opposing the smelter (KOSU). So the state’s lawyer and the incoming governor are on opposite sides of a project that taxpayers are helping pay for.

For PSO customers: the Corporation Commission voted 2–1 for a permanent 3.55% rate increase, starting with December bills. It replaces the $11-a-month interim charge in place since July, and residential customers get no refund of what they paid under it. Industrial customers get bill credits within 90 days. Commission Chair Kim David voted no (News On 6). Large-load industrial rates are up in January, and that’s when we’ll see who pays for the data-center and smelter load. Separately, Oklahoma Voice reports 17 rural hospitals are at immediate risk of closing as Medicaid money shrinks. That’s their count, and I haven’t checked it against a primary list.

Faith, order, money, power

Trump on Truth Social Thursday: “We don’t need a deal with Iran. Those days have long since passed. … We’ve got their money, Hundreds of Billions of Dollars, and we’ve got their Strait!” He also took Vice President Vance off the Iran talks and told him to focus on “corruption and crime” in Democratic-run states (CNBC). Note the timing: this comes nine days after midterm losses that Republicans themselves blamed on the war. Meanwhile the strait is not “ours.” Iran claims four tankers hit mines Wednesday and Thursday on routes it calls unauthorized. UKMTO separately confirmed a tanker hit by an unidentified projectile northwest of Ras al-Khaimah, with an engine-room fire and the crew evacuated. Reuters couldn’t verify Iran’s account. At least 10 ships were hit in the first 10 days of November, after 37 in October (Washington Institute data via Reuters). Speaker Ghalibaf told Al Jazeera the waterway will “never return” to its pre-war status. Both capitals are now saying they can outwait the other. Shippers and drivers pay the cost of that standoff.

Economics and geopolitics

Treasury’s October statement, which is primary data: the government took in $369 billion, spent $773 billion, and ran a $404 billion deficit. That’s a record for October and 42% worse than a year ago. About $119 billion of it is a calendar quirk: November benefit payments went out in October because Nov. 1 fell on a weekend. Even so, Treasury puts the adjusted deficit at $285 billion, up 49%. Tariff receipts fell 39% to $19.2 billion. Interest on the debt was $115 billion in one month (Reuters). The government borrowed $308 billion from the public to cover it. Trump’s line that the budget “could be quickly BALANCED” by fixing blue-state fraud doesn’t hold up against these numbers.

The bond market noticed. The 10-year Treasury yield rose about 12 basis points to 5.11%, and the 2-year rose about 14 to 4.87%. A $25 billion 30-year bond auction drew weak demand: bids covered it 2.36 times, against a recent average of 2.45. Fed presidents Hammack, Goolsbee, and Daly all backed more hikes, and futures now treat a December 9 hike as close to certain (Barchart; Reuters). Mortgages are already showing it. NAR says October existing-home sales fell to an annual pace of 3.75 million, the lowest since August 2010 (via CoinDesk). Brent jumped 3.3% to $95.82 on the Iran post and on Houthi missiles aimed at western Saudi Arabia. Gold fell about 3% to about $3,877.

In AI money: Nvidia’s 8-K confirms Jensen Huang resigned Wednesday as CEO and from the board, effective immediately. CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO, and longtime director Harvey Jones is now chair. The filing gives no reason. Earlier reporting tied the board’s talks to Huang’s admitted affair with a woman US officials suspect is a Chinese intelligence asset, which is reporting, not a finding (CoinDesk). Nvidia closed down 4.3%, after being down as much as 14%. The president then publicly named Tim Cook as the man to replace Huang. A sitting president picking the CEO of the most important US chip company tells you how tightly the government and that company are now tied together.

Bitcoin

Down roughly 3% to about $71,000. US spot ETFs lost $300 million Wednesday, $270 million of it from IBIT, for a fifth straight day of outflows and $1.44 billion over five days (SoSoValue via Bitcoin.com). Some of that is large holders moving coins into ETFs in kind rather than selling, so the cash-flow numbers overstate the exit. The structural news: MSCI confirmed it will drop Strategy, the largest corporate bitcoin holder, from its global indexes after the Nov. 30 close under its new “non-operating companies” rule (Reuters; CoinDesk). That forces index funds to sell, and it tells you the index providers see a bitcoin treasury company as a fund, not a business. With rate hikes coming, bitcoin is trading with risk assets, not acting as a refuge.
