Tulsa and Oklahoma

The long-promised Tulsa–Jenks low-water dam is short on money. According to the Tulsa Flyer’s account of a Nov. 9 town hall, Tulsa, Jenks, the Muscogee Nation and INCOG have pledged about $105 million, including about $62.2 million from Tulsa’s Vision 2016 sales tax. The four versions on the table cost $143.5 million (dam only) to $194 million (the plan voters approved, with parks and a pedestrian bridge). That leaves a gap of roughly $38 million to $89 million, depending on how much gets cut. Officials are looking at Tulsa’s leftover Vision economic-development money, private donors and the state. If the dam dies, officials say the money is already spoken for: Jenks shifts its share to other city infrastructure, and Tulsa must put about $18 million into a Zink Dam maintenance endowment and split the rest among river projects. A steering committee meets in early December. The question for taxpayers: voters approved a $194 million project in 2016, and the cheaper versions drop the amenities they were promised. Council takes up the Peak on the Parkway tax-increment hearing Wednesday at 5 p.m.

The new Legislature is taking shape. Seven new senators, all Republicans, took the oath Monday and officially take office Wednesday. The Senate stays 40–8. House Republicans nominated Speaker Kyle Hilbert of Bristow for another term in a closed caucus. The full House votes Jan. 5, and members are sworn in Tuesday. House Democrats picked Rep. Melissa Provenzano of Tulsa as leader after gaining six seats. Gov.-elect Mike Mazzei’s first hire is state Superintendent Lindel Fields as chief of staff, plus a statewide literacy director, Rebecca Pellam. He also named Walters-era allies Suzanne Reynolds and Everett Piper to education transition roles, with his brother Dan leading the transition team. Mazzei says Fields will also help push income-tax elimination and ending property taxes for seniors and veterans. Watch whether a bill and a revenue plan follow.

The state agriculture department says USDA confirmed bird flu in an Oklahoma dairy herd, location not released. It says pasteurized milk is safe. It’s the state’s first confirmed case since January in Wagoner County. Tulsa police were searching Monday evening for a man who exchanged gunfire with an officer near 64th and South Peoria. The officer wasn’t hurt.

Faith, order, money, power

The Nvidia scandal produced its first government action. Commerce’s Bureau of Industry and Security issued an interim final rule Monday. It requires a license for overseas data centers using advanced US chips to sell significant computing power to companies headquartered in China (or Macau and other countries of concern). Data centers already doing that have 90 days from the effective date to get one. The rule also requires a license to ship advanced chips to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, which the rule calls higher-than-normal diversion risks. Per BIS’s release as reported by CNBC, the rule was “written by and at the direction of” Treasury and the White House National Security Council. That’s unusual, and it tells you Commerce was overruled. Treasury Secretary Bessent said Friday that Treasury, the Pentagon, State and the US Trade Representative had wanted Megaspeed blacklisted for months and Commerce hadn’t acted. He’s now calling for an outside CEO at Nvidia. Incentive: every agency wants to be the one that was right before the scandal. Lutnick’s Commerce Department is the one that has to explain why it waited.

Iran-US tensions keep building in the Gulf. The USS Theodore Roosevelt entered Central Command’s region Monday, making three US carrier groups there (AP). UK maritime monitors (UKMTO) say the Panama-flagged On Phoenix was hit by an unknown projectile about three nautical miles off Oman while leaving Hormuz. It had an engine-room fire, and the crew is safe. That’s the first named ship in days of Iranian claims. The Revolutionary Guard’s claims of tankers sinking after hitting mines still have no hull name, and CENTCOM still says the main lanes are clear. Iran says there will be no UN inspections of damaged nuclear sites until the war is “permanently ended” (CBS). The Trump–Xi–Putin meeting in Shenzhen is still set for Thursday.

Security watch

Microsoft 365, new facts: cause found. Microsoft’s preliminary post-incident report, reproduced by Office Watch and summarized by Ars Technica, says three things combined. First, a gateway code defect shipped in July went undetected because the test hardware had fewer CPU cores than production machines. Second, Teams traffic was shifted onto the primary gateway about 15 hours before the outage. Third, the gateway fleet was already running 14% below normal capacity. More than 80% of affected gateway servers froze. Restarts and rollbacks didn’t work because the bug was already in the earlier version. The outage ran from 10:20 a.m. CT Nov. 12 to 10:10 a.m. CT Nov. 13. Microsoft lists the final status as “not related to cyber or AI/agents.” Still open: why the regional isolation failed. The final report is due Nov. 20. Why it matters: one dormant bug took down email for much of the working world for a day, with no attacker involved.

FAA ground stop. No root cause yet. Secretary Duffy told ABC that 69 of the new routers are in the airspace system and all have broadcasting turned off. The FAA’s written Q&A says about 60. He said the open question is “user error or installer error from L3Harris” versus a faulty router. HPE, which owns router maker Juniper, says the device “functioned as it was configured to by the installer.” That puts the blame back on the contractor’s setup. Still open: the lab finding, and whether Congress holds a hearing.

Chase slowdown. JPMorgan Chase said a temporary software bug in its own on-premise systems slowed consumer transactions Monday. It said the problem wasn’t cyber-related, customers kept access to their funds, and it aimed to resolve it by day’s end (company statement via AP and CNBC). Customers at other banks saw delays only on payments touching Chase, including some Zelle transfers. It’s the third big infrastructure failure in five days, and none so far traces to an attacker.

Economics

Stocks fell for a third straight day: S&P 500 −1.9% to 7,416.59, Nasdaq −2.5%, Dow −739 points (CNBC). Nvidia fell 4.1% to about $156 after a CNBC source said the board isn’t considering a Huang return and the BIS rule landed. The chip index fell 5.3%. Nvidia reports Tuesday after the close, about 3:20 p.m. CT. The New York Fed’s Empire State factory index fell to 7.8, still growth but the weakest in months. US crude settled at $85.72, down 3%, its lowest since late August. Brent was $92.44. The 10-year Treasury yield eased to 5.12% as money moved into safety. Fed Chair Warsh spoke only about AI: it could lift productivity, or displace workers, or fail to pay off, and the Fed is modeling all three. He said nothing about rates. In plain terms: cheaper oil helps at the pump, but borrowing is still expensive, and the market is pricing the AI build-out as a riskier bet than it did a week ago.

Bitcoin

Bitcoin is about $65,000, steady while the Nasdaq fell, after Sunday’s dip below $62,000 on forced selling. Strategy bought 485 more bitcoin. That’s price action, not policy or protocol.

Sports
