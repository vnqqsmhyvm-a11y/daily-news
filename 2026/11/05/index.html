Tulsa and Oklahoma

The Corporation Commission voted 2–1 today to approve Public Service Company of Oklahoma’s rate case (PUD2025-000075). Commissioners Brian Bingman and Todd Hiett carried it; Chair Kim David cast the no. Per David, the order lands a 3.55% overall residential increase — not stacked on top of the interim rates already in effect since July, but enough that residential customers will pay “just a little bit more” than they are now, with no refund of the roughly $11-a-month interim they have already paid. Industrial customers do get refunds as bill credits within 90 days. That sits above the Bingman draft that had residential in the high-1% to mid-2% range and well above the settlement PSO, the Attorney General, AARP and the commission’s Public Utility Division had backed (about 1% residential). PSO’s spokesman said the company is “concerned” and calculating bill impacts; the final published numbers are due in about six days. All three commissioners did approve a large-load tariff for customers over 75 megawatts (data centers and the like), with more work left on “bring your own generation” so those loads do not free-ride the grid. The tell is unchanged: who pays for the buildout that grows the rate base. Reporting is from the Tulsa Flyer; the signed order text had not been read independently by press time.

Tuesday’s statewide results are mostly in, still unofficial until county boards certify Friday and the State Election Board meets Nov. 10. Republican Mike Mazzei won the governorship with about 52% to Democrat Cyndi Munson’s 45.9% — the closest a Democrat has come in decades, and Munson carried Tulsa County. Republicans swept the other statewide races (T.W. Shannon for lieutenant governor, Jon Echols for attorney general, Kevin Hern for the open U.S. Senate seat) and kept every U.S. House seat. Both state questions failed: SQ 845 (Judicial Nominating Commission rewrite) lost about 72.6%–27.4%; SQ 847 (tighter property-assessment caps) failed about 54.3%–45.7%. Inside the city, Proposition 1 raising the hotel/motel guest tax from 5% to 9.9% passed about 57%–43% (city’s projection: $10.4 million a year rising to $20.6 million, effective Jan. 1, for BOK Center and Arvest Convention Center work plus tourism marketing). District 1 kept Vanessa Hall-Harper by 216 votes. In the Legislature, Republicans kept their supermajorities, but Democrats flipped five House seats — including Tulsa County’s T.J. Marti — and picked up an open Lawton seat, taking the House Democratic caucus from 20 to 26. Censured Rep. Danny Williams (R-Seminole) still won with about 62%.

National and foreign

Democrats took both chambers of Congress in Tuesday’s midterms; the new majorities seat in January. Vice President JD Vance said bluntly that “the reason we lost is the war,” tying the rout to the Iran conflict and high fuel costs — words, not a policy change. Watch what is done: UK maritime reporting still shows tanker incidents near a record pace (at least five vessels flagged since Sunday), Iran’s Revolutionary Guard and army claimed multiple tankers and a tug caught fire after mine strikes in Hormuz waters Thursday, and UKMTO separately reported a tanker hit by projectiles off Qatar. Pentagon officials told the New York Times that U.S. air and naval forces remain staged for resumed major combat on the president’s order. Qatar keeps mediating; Iran’s supreme leader said there should not be “even the slightest inclination towards submission.” Eurasia Group cut its odds of a deal and raised the chance of a prolonged standoff. The incentive on both sides: fuel prices at the pump shaped the midterm, and neither side has yet traded steel for a signed settlement.

Economics and geopolitics

Wall Street erased Wednesday’s post-election bounce and then some. The Nasdaq fell about 5.5% into correction territory, the S&P 500 about 4.4%, and the Dow about 3.4% (~1,833 points) — the worst day for the three since the April 2025 tariff shock — led by AI and semiconductor names as investors priced a possible slowdown in data-center capex after Democrats took Congress. Nvidia alone lost more than $440 billion in market value on the day; the “Magnificent Seven” shed about $1 trillion combined. Oil bounced off Wednesday’s >8% collapse: Brent settled at $98.29 (+3.8%) and WTI at $90.52 (+2.7%), still well above pre-war levels. Weekly jobless claims fell 6,000 to 189,000, matching a 57-year low and under the ~196,000 estimate. That is one week of claims, not the labor market. Friday’s October payrolls (consensus still around 65,000) is the number that will move Fed pricing; futures still lean toward a December hike even after the midterms and the oil drop. The 10-year Treasury eased into the mid-4.8%s from election-night highs near 4.95%.

Bitcoin

Bitcoin gave back Wednesday’s rebound above $80,000 and traded under $76,000 Thursday as risk assets sold off with tech. U.S. spot bitcoin ETFs had taken in about $74.6 million Wednesday (Farside), ending a three-day outflow streak, but that did not hold the price once equities cracked. Policy, not just tape: Stifel’s Washington desk said the stalled Clarity Act is “off the table” for the lame-duck session, with low odds of a bipartisan deal next Congress and Sen. Elizabeth Warren expected to chair Senate Banking. Coins left on an exchange remain only as safe as that exchange; the Binance hack hangover is still in the room.
