Tulsa and Oklahoma

It’s Election Day, and polls closed at 7 p.m. with no results yet as this went up. Statewide, voters pick a governor (Republican Mike Mazzei, Democrat Cyndi Munson, three independents), an open U.S. Senate seat (Republican Kevin Hern against Democrat N’Kiyla Jasmine Thomas and three others), attorney general, superintendent, treasurer, labor, insurance and corporation commissioners, nine appellate judges up for retention, and two state questions: SQ 845 restructures the Judicial Nominating Commission, and SQ 847 slows how fast assessed property values can rise. Inside the city, the hotel/motel tax question would take the rate from 5% to 9.9% (the city’s own projection was $10.4 million a year rising to $20.6 million, backing $80 million in bonds), and District 1 settles the Hall-Harper versus Wilson runoff. Tulsa County’s 26,211 early voters beat both the 2022 midterm (16,302) and the 2024 presidential (25,954); the election board credits the two new early-voting sites, so read that as access as much as enthusiasm. About a dozen of the county’s 251 precincts had machine failures this morning, which the board says were fixed or swapped; ballots cast while a scanner was down go into the sealed emergency bin and get counted once it’s running, which is standard procedure, not a lost vote. The Justice Department also sent federal monitors to Tulsa, Oklahoma and Muskogee counties; they have to stay outside and 300 feet back. That’s reporting from Tulsa World, Tulsa Flyer, FOX23 and News On 6, not county records.

The other Tulsa story today is your power bill. The Corporation Commission’s 1:30 p.m. agenda had a possible vote on PSO’s rate case (PUD2025-000075). The draft from Commissioner Bingman sets a floor of at least a 1.5% total-bill increase for every customer class, puts residential somewhere in the high-1% to mid-2% range, approves a separate tariff for loads over 75 megawatts like data centers, and makes PSO refund the part of its interim rates that came in above the final number within 90 days. Chairman Kim David pushed back last week, saying she wanted more numbers. No vote result had been reported by press time, so treat the order as pending until the signed text is out. The tell when it is: the settlement PSO signed with the AG and AARP had residential near 5%. If the final lands around 2%, the opening ask was padded, and the large-load terms decide who carries the data-center buildout.

National and foreign

The U.S. hit Iran’s Qeshm Island on Monday, according to U.S. officials speaking to the Wall Street Journal, which they described as a strike on surface-to-air missiles aimed at U.S. assets. It’s one of several limited strikes kept deliberately quiet, all while the White House says a deal “could happen” and Qatar keeps shuttling proposals. Watch what’s being done, not what’s said: strikes are continuing during the talks, Iran’s foreign ministry says it won’t negotiate under coercion and wants the U.S. naval blockade lifted, and the UK’s maritime agency counts at least four attacks on ships in Gulf waters since Nov. 1. Last week’s reporting said Washington told allies to prepare for possible strikes after the election. Tomorrow is the first day that window is open, so the next week will show whether the talks were real or just a pause for the midterms.

Money and energy

Oil fell for a second day. Brent settled at $103.60 (down $1.17, its lowest close since Oct. 7) and WTI at $96.09. That’s still far above the $60s and $70s before the war started in February. Aramco’s chief executive said today that Hormuz shipments are still below pre-war levels and that some published counts double-count crude moved between tankers outside the strait, so be skeptical of “flows are back” headlines. Treasury yields jumped: the 10-year rose about 6 basis points to 4.95%, its biggest one-day gain since late September, and the 30-year reached 5.32%. Mortgages are priced off that 10-year, so any relief on home loans is moving away again. The reason was a strong September job-openings report from the Labor Department: 7.52 million openings against about 7.1 million expected, with layoffs at their lowest in a year and a half. That number is two months old and gets revised, so the real test is Friday’s October payrolls, where forecasts center around 65,000. Stocks were mixed: the S&P 500 rose 0.4%, the Nasdaq 0.8% on a chip rebound, and the Dow fell 0.4% as Apple dropped about 2% on a weak margin forecast. Gold fell to $3,927.90, within about 1% of its low for the year, so with war, deficits and 5% long bonds all in play, gold isn’t acting as a safe haven right now.

Bitcoin

Bitcoin traded around $77,000 and is on track for a fifth straight weekly decline. Money is still leaving after the Binance hack: U.S. spot bitcoin funds lost about $1.04 billion over Friday and Monday, according to Bloomberg’s tally, including $405 million out of BlackRock’s fund Monday, its biggest one-day outflow since June. Binance’s CEO says customers pulled $5.28 billion more than they deposited in the 46 hours after it reopened, but deposits were slightly ahead of withdrawals by early Tuesday. That’s his data, not an independent audit. A second exchange, Brazil’s Mercado Bitcoin, lost about $46 million from hot wallets Monday. On the other side, Strategy bought 811 more bitcoin last week at about $80,800 each and now holds 849,327. It paid for them by selling its own stock. The lesson keeps repeating: coins left on an exchange are only as safe as that exchange.

Sports

Oklahoma State wrestling opened by winning 9 of 10 weights at the first Cowboy Invitational in Gallagher-Iba, and its first dual is Drexel on Nov. 8. OU wrestling opened with a 46–0 shutout of Northeastern State.
