Tulsa and Oklahoma

Mazzei’s $11 million self-loan: what’s on paper and what isn’t. Rep. Jared Deck (D-Norman) on Thursday sent letters to the FDIC, the Oklahoma State Banking Department, and the Ethics Commission. He’s asking them to look at a Regent Bank loan to Mike Mazzei, the Republican nominee for governor, and at the roughly $11.6 million Mazzei has lent his own campaign, which was about 88% of the campaign’s money going into the August runoff. Here’s the record so far. A financing statement filed Jan. 13 with the Oklahoma County clerk names Mazzei as debtor and Regent as secured party, with his shares in Tulsa Wealth Advisors (Trinity Strategic Wealth) as collateral. It shows no amount, rate, or terms, and nothing ties it to the campaign loans, which are reported as coming from “self” at zero interest. Deck says he hasn’t verified any link and isn’t alleging a crime. Regent’s CEO, Sean Kouplen, sat in Stitt’s first cabinet alongside Mazzei. The campaign called it Democrats “falsely pulling fire alarms” and didn’t answer the question about the loan terms.

Both sides have a motive here. Deck is a Democrat going public 18 days before the election about a filing that’s been public for nine months. Mazzei is asking voters to trust him without saying who he owes. The gap in disclosure is real and isn’t partisan, though. Rep. Jonathan Wilk (R-Goldsby) separately wants the Ethics Commission to review any candidate loan over $100,000, and the commission has put that on its rulemaking agenda. The next campaign reports are due Oct. 26, covering Aug. 11 through Oct. 19. (OK Politics Today; county financing statement as described there, not read directly. Reporting.)

TU cuts about 9% of its staff to deal with its debt

The University of Tulsa is cutting just under 9% of its staff effective Nov. 1, mostly in central administration. Many of the cuts are vacancies that won’t be filled, and no faculty jobs are affected. The university is carrying about $126 million in debt after a credit downgrade, and it projects positive cash flow this year for the first time since fiscal 2021. In August it announced it would cut sticker tuition from $54,000 to $25,000, and it’s aiming to grow from about 4,000 students to 10,000 within a decade. That’s a bet on volume made with borrowed money. The city’s stake is Gilcrease. The city owns the museum and TU manages it, the reopening is set for March, and the management agreement comes up for renegotiation in 2028. President Leeds says TU isn’t walking away. (Tulsa Flyer; Public Radio Tulsa, whose license TU owns; News On 6 for the university’s statement. Reporting.)

The state is backfilling a solar tax break it already voted to end. At a House budget interim study Thursday, Rep. Mike Dobrinski (R-Okeene) said Oklahoma has more than 100 solar and battery-storage applications, about 17.6 gigawatts or 43% of the state’s proposed queue, that could claim the five-year manufacturing property-tax exemption from 1985. Lawmakers passed SB 237 to take solar and batteries out of that exemption, and Stitt pocket-vetoed it. Counties and schools don’t eat the cost. The state reimburses them out of a fund that has been short every year since about 2003, and the 2026 top-up was nearly $65 million from general revenue. In practice it’s a subsidy that gets billed to state taxpayers, and the state’s definition of “manufacturer” has grown well past factories. This was a study with no bill attached. A real fix would need a constitutional change. (KGOU/KOSU on the interim study; House fiscal staff figures. Reporting.)

1921 graves search finds 32 more grave shafts

The city’s sixth field season at Oaklawn has turned up 32 grave shafts that weren’t in any city record, including six outside the west fence line under a trail, and three individuals were recovered Oct. 13. Fieldwork ends later this month and lab work continues in November. (State Archaeologist via FOX23/KRMG. Reporting.)

Gas dips under $4, but diesel doesn't

AAA on Oct. 16 has Oklahoma regular at $3.993, down from $4.053 a week ago, and diesel at $5.910, near the state record of $6.029 set Sept. 22. Tulsa regular is $3.954 and diesel $5.906. The national average for regular is $4.327. A year ago Oklahoma regular was $2.565. Friday’s crude jump hasn’t reached the pump yet. (AAA, primary.)

National and foreign

Hormuz attacks hit a record week, and oil is moving less of it. BBC Verify’s count of UK Maritime Trade Operations (UKMTO) reports shows 12 ships attacked Oct. 5–11, the most in any week of the war, with six on a single day. Kpler has flows through the strait at 12.6 million barrels a day on Oct. 13, down from about 15 million at the end of September and against a prewar level near 17 million. Iran says it’s escalating in response to the U.S. blockade of its ports and last month’s sanctions. A Saudi-led coalition says it shot down a ballistic missile aimed at Yanbu, the Red Sea port Riyadh uses to get oil around Hormuz. Unverified Israeli reports of an Iranian preemptive strike are just rumor until something happens. Ukraine has also gone back to hitting Russian refineries. One thing to watch is Thursday’s report that Washington is weighing easing restrictions on the “shadow fleet” tankers. That would be sanctions imposed but not enforced, because the barrels are needed. (BBC Verify/UKMTO, Kpler, and Reuters. Reporting; the Israeli strike claim is speculation.)

Oil is back over $100

Brent settled at $109.86, up 3.7% and its highest close since May 19. Front-month WTI closed at $101.41, its first finish above $100 in a month. Diesel was already the weak spot. Thursday’s wholesale inflation report showed energy feeding into prices, and this move lands on top of that. (Reuters/WSJ settlements. Fact.)

Warsh gave no rate signal, and markets read that as a pause

Speaking at the IMF meetings in Bangkok, the Fed chair said “I don’t prejudge meetings” and that the Fed is “not in that business anymore,” meaning it won’t give forward guidance. He called the current wave of central-bank tightening around the world “synchronous,” with “no free riders,” and said AI’s path will be “very bumpy.” CME odds of holding at the Oct. 27–28 meeting rose to about 72%, and most traders still expect a hike in December. Those were words. The action on record is still September’s hike to 3.75%–4.00%. The IMF says global public debt is on track to pass 100% of world GDP, a level last seen after World War II, and the administration says it will grow its way out. That’s a claim until the deficits actually shrink. (CNBC from the event; CME FedWatch. Fact / reporting.)

AI debt worries pushed stocks down while bonds rallied

The S&P 500 fell about 1.1% to 7,659 and the Nasdaq 2.1% to 26,140, and the Dow slipped 0.3% to 52,002. That capped the worst week for stocks since late June. AP’s read is that investors doubt AI companies financing their buildout with debt can keep borrowing with yields at multi-year highs. That would be credit stress showing up where the bubble talk has been. The 10-year Treasury yield is near 5.10%, about 15 basis points lower on the week, but the real (inflation-adjusted) 10-year yield is still 2.78%. Freddie Mac’s 30-year mortgage rate is unchanged at 7.40%. December gold is about $4,187. Bitcoin is about $81,700 and drifting with stocks, with no policy or protocol news. Spot bitcoin ETFs had about $146 million in net outflows for the week. (AP; FRED; Yahoo/exchange prints; ETF flows per Bitcoin.com, unchecked. Fact / reporting.)
