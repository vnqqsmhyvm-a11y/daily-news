Tulsa and Oklahoma

Early voting for the Nov. 3 general opens tomorrow. Polls run 8 a.m. to 6 p.m. Wednesday through Friday and 8 a.m. to 2 p.m. Saturday, Halloween, at four sites in Tulsa County. Statewide there are two questions. SQ 845 changes who sits on the Judicial Nominating Commission and how they’re picked. SQ 847 lowers property-tax assessment caps starting in 2027. Your sample ballot on the OK Voter Portal is the source of truth.

The money in the governor’s race is lopsided. Republican Mike Mazzei has now loaned his own campaign nearly $13 million, a record for an Oklahoma governor’s race. He’s raised about $3.5 million from donors and had about $1.6 million on hand as of Oct. 19 (Oklahoma Voice, from filed reports). Democrat Cyndi Munson’s latest report couldn’t be checked because the Ethics Commission’s system was down Tuesday. The job pays $185,000 a year. Mazzei calls the loans a “good investment” in Oklahoma. That’s his stated reason; what return a $13 million bet on a $185,000 office is meant to buy is the open question.

In Tulsa’s District 1 council runoff, Councilor Vanessa Hall-Harper says a group called A Strong Oklahoma is running TV ads, mailers and a website against her and for challenger Corinice Wilson. Its funders are undisclosed. Wilson denies any connection (Tulsa Flyer). One mailer blames Hall-Harper for “higher utility bills because of the new data center.” The city council doesn’t set PSO’s rates; the Corporation Commission does. The data-center rate fight has reached a city race whether or not the charge fits the office.

License-plate cameras are on two tracks. At the city’s first public-safety community conversation, Police Chief Jonathan Brooks said Flock cameras are used to investigate crimes, not to write tickets, and that the city has narrowed how widely it shares camera data (News On 6). Three more sessions run through Nov. 5. At the Capitol, the Senate interim study led by Sen. Mary Boren is aimed at the six words “or as otherwise permitted by law.” That clause sits at the end of a state law that otherwise limits plate-reader data to enforcing car insurance (The Oklahoman, via OK Policy’s daily roundup; the full story wasn’t read). So far this is talk. A bill filed for the February session would be the real step.

Lawmakers’ Joint Committee on State-Tribal Relations voted 6–1 to give Tulsa County DA Steve Kunzweiler another $500,000 from the state’s $10 million State-Tribal Litigation Fund. The money is for his fight with the Muscogee Nation over whether the state can prosecute non-Muscogee tribal citizens for minor crimes on the reservation. The Tenth Circuit hears the case Nov. 17 (KOSU).

The Frontier and ProPublica report that Citation Oil & Gas appears more than 1,000 times over the past decade in the Corporation Commission’s database of pollution reports and rule violations, more than any other operator. The commission has no record of ever fining it. Staff built a case and recommended punishment, but agency leaders let the company resume injection at higher pressures than staff advised (The Frontier). Free markets assume an operator pays for the damage it does. A regulator that won’t enforce shifts those costs onto neighbors and landowners. That’s reporting; the commission’s own records weren’t checked here.

The State Department of Education is asking for a $123 million increase for fiscal 2028, citing insurance costs, regional training centers and literacy coaching (KOCO). It’s an ask, not an appropriation.

Faith, order, money, power

No new US strike on Iran has been ordered. CNN, citing two officials, reports that forces are ready to start major operations within 96 hours of an order. CNN also reports, from an Israeli source, that Trump turned down Netanyahu’s push for a joint strike before Israel’s elections; Netanyahu’s office calls that fake. Trump has said there won’t be strikes before Election Day. The planners quoted say it’s “when, not if.” That timing is the incentive to watch, since the midterms are a week out. At a Corpus Christi rally Tuesday, Trump called attacking Iran “one of the greatest things I ever did.”

The harder fact came from the UN. IAEA chief Rafael Grossi told the Security Council the agency can’t verify that Iran hasn’t diverted nuclear material, including 440 kg of highly enriched uranium. Iran keeps refusing inspectors access to the Isfahan enrichment plant (Fox live coverage, quoting Grossi’s statement).

Diplomacy is moving too. Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan met Iranian officials in Tehran on Monday and agreed to keep talking. Iran’s Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei said the “neither war nor peace” state has to end, while insisting on control of the Strait of Hormuz. The Houthis claimed a missile strike on Saudi Arabia’s Ras Tanura refinery. A fire was reportedly contained, and there’s no independent confirmation of damage. Iran’s President Masoud Pezeshkian met Putin in Moscow.

Economics in plain language

Oil fell for a second day on the diplomacy headlines. December WTI settled at $99.90, down $1.86 and back under $100. December Brent settled at $113.88, down $1.93. Diesel futures dropped about 14 cents to $4.78 a gallon (DTN; CNBC and Reuters match). The physical market hasn’t eased the way prices have. Ship tracking cited by DTN shows Persian Gulf crude exports at about 65% of pre-war levels, down from 90% at the start of October. UBS raised its year-end Brent call to about $110 from $100; that’s a forecast.

The Fed’s two-day meeting began. The decision comes Wednesday at 1 p.m. Central. Futures put the odds of holding at 3.75% to 4% at about 92%, after September’s hike (CNBC). The thing to watch is whether Chair Warsh signals more hikes. The 10-year Treasury yield sits at 4.99% and the 30-year at 5.31%. The 2-year is at 4.74% and has risen three days running. The data came in firm. September durable-goods orders rose 1.8% to $345.4 billion, and 0.5% excluding transportation (Census). The Conference Board’s consumer confidence index jumped to 91.7 from 84.2 as gas prices eased, but consumers said they plan to spend less on services and big-ticket goods. Treasury also ran a $6 billion buyback of long bonds. That manages how easily its debt trades; it doesn’t shrink the debt.

The AI buildout keeps running on credit. Bank of America, Citi and Morgan Stanley (first lien) and Blackstone (second lien) committed $63 billion to a special-purpose company. It will buy Broadcom AI chips and networking gear and lease them to Anthropic, with deliveries starting in 2027. Broadcom is guaranteeing the hardware’s resale value up to the size of the first-lien loans, with peak exposure expected around $43 billion (Broadcom release). In plain terms, the chipmaker is backstopping the value of its own product so its customer can borrow to buy it. That’s vendor financing. If AI chips lose value faster than planned, Broadcom absorbs the loss. Stocks liked it: the S&P 500 rose 0.6% to 7,722.60 and the Nasdaq rose 1.2%.

Sports
